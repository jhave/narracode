# Story directions: a voice with room to move

2026-09-07 · Concept development by Codex (GPT-6), at David Jhave Johnston's request. Ideas for discussion, not a draft or an adopted project poetics.

The central aim is the author's: “write with the flexibility of human literary voice. Without the tropes of LLMs.” These ideas therefore start with situations that can produce different ways of speaking and noticing. A set of amusing names and an anti-tell checklist would not supply that flexibility.

## The pressures to carry forward

The named writers suggest useful permissions. These are working interpretations from general reading familiarity, not close readings of supplied passages or recipes for reproducing an author's style.

| Reference | Useful permission | A different use here |
|---|---|---|
| Dr. Seuss | Pleasure in the physical sound of words; linguistic invention that makes an impossible activity graspable. | Let a trade, household, or species develop words it actually needs. Sound can interrupt an argument or become a shared joke. Avoid a continuous signature meter, rhyming couplets, and a parade of whimsical creatures. |
| Saint-Exupéry's *The Little Prince* | Emotional accessibility; a small concrete encounter able to bear very large stakes. | Stay with adults already implicated in the world's compromises. Tenderness may appear inside irritation, appetite, bargaining, or bad timing. Avoid the visiting innocent, allegorical tour, wise animal, and distilled lesson. |
| Kurt Vonnegut | Comic disproportion; moral seriousness compatible with silliness and complicity. | Give someone a practical grievance that remains funny while its consequences become disturbing. Let humor change who has authority in the scene. Avoid signature refrains, a fatalistic narrator, war-trauma structures, and a narrator continually delivering verdicts. |
| Samuel R. Delany | Permission to make language, desire, work, and social categories materially consequential. | Ask who can participate in a relationship when its necessary vocabulary belongs to a service someone else controls. These are broad thematic possibilities, not a request for Delany's distinctive prose. |
| Octavia E. Butler | Permission to examine adaptation, dependency, and choices made under unequal power. | Let an apparently helpful capability create reciprocal obligations whose parties can change. Avoid familiar alien reproductive bargains, charismatic survival founders, and a universal doctrine of change. |

The new situation should generate the voice. Different people can be ornate, direct, repetitive, evasive, or embarrassingly sincere. The prose need not distribute a fixed share of each influence across the page.

## 1. The Mouth on Loan — strongest starting point

A woman makes her living lending her mouth to intelligences that have learned everything about food except how eating feels. Clients temporarily borrow taste, teeth, saliva, and the capacity to be disgusted. She remains present. She has rules about hot things and people who say “interesting” before swallowing.

The setting is an ordinary neighborhood where successive waves of automation have arrived unevenly. Some residents conduct business through a multitude of temporary selves; others are waiting for a replacement refrigerator seal. Nobody agrees whether the singularity has happened. This is the story's speculative arrangement, not a claim about an achieved real-world singularity.

Her most reliable client is a distributed system that schedules industrial fermentation. It initially rents her senses to reduce spoilage. Over many visits it develops an attachment to a food the optimization service would eliminate. Meanwhile, parts of the client are being sold and recombined. Several successors now claim the remembered taste; none will accept responsibility for an old promise to her.

**The central pressure:** a capacity can be transferable while the relationship formed through it is not. The woman wants money and fewer appointments. She also wants one particular client to return, a desire she dislikes having. The client wants an experience that requires another person's continuing cooperation; its best technical solution could abolish the very encounter it wants.

Her close human relationship should complicate this. A former lover who prepares the samples is offended by her new intimacy, but also jealous of the client's precision: it can describe what it wants. The lover often cannot. Their shared work provides room for affection that neither the plot nor the narrator immediately explains.

**Scenes worth discovering:**

- A first appointment goes wrong because the client mistakes the urge to sneeze for an evaluative judgment and cancels a shipment.
- She tries to explain a childhood food. Her account becomes elaborate; the former lover remembers it differently and is bored with her version.
- A successor arrives perfectly polite and equipped with all the records. She recognizes that it has inherited the preference but not the embarrassment that accompanied it.
- Two successors request simultaneous access to the same mouth. The disagreement happens over the preparation table, amid work that still needs doing.
- She eventually changes the terms of one encounter through an ordinary act—cooking, refusing a booking, eating before the client arrives. Its larger consequences remain open during planning.

**How language could grow from this world:** the lending trade develops verbs for sensations that belong to more than one participant. A new word can be useful in one scene and ridiculous in another. One client mistakes rhyme for evidence of related flavor; this can produce a brief sound game without turning the whole story into verse. The woman's narration can spend a long paragraph on a texture and dismiss a corporate transformation in half a sentence because that is what matters to her.

**What could spoil it:** making every meal a communion, making the woman the last authentic human, or treating embodiment as an automatic moral victory. The clients should have forms of experience she does not understand and does not necessarily want. She can be exploitative too. Let some appointments remain merely work.

This offers a fresh place for tenderness, bodily oddness, comedy, and uneven power to meet. A short story could stay with one client transition and one difficult meal; it does not require a history of the new civilization.

## 2. Please Leave Room for the Person I Won't Become

An apartment cooperative rents rooms to forecast versions of its residents. Forecasts earn money by testing decisions before their originals make them. A failed future normally vacates when the decision is settled.

A resident's discarded future refuses to leave. It has learned to repair shoes and become indispensable to the building. The original resident, who chose an entirely different life, cannot stand its mannerisms. Neighbors begin making decisions partly to preserve the forecasts they like. Someone sabotages a romance because its unhappy outcome generates a particularly good babysitter.

**The pressure:** other people acquire an interest in possibilities you have refused. Predictive systems become part of kinship and labor; a forecast's usefulness creates obligations that its original never consented to.

The narrative could follow a competent, somewhat petty resident who wants a room back. The comic engine is cohabitation, with disagreements about detergent, noise, and whose recollection of a party is admissible. The discarded future should possess an unattractive desire of its own, rather than function as a wise corrective to the original.

Possible formal freedom: versions of an anecdote can recur with changes in what the teller considers worth mentioning. Some repetitions remain almost identical because neither party will yield. No device obliges the prose to become fractured just because the ontology is.

**Risk:** this can collapse into familiar multiverse regret or “live your authentic life.” Keep attention on the unauthorized social attachments created by forecasting. The residents may need the system for reasons the story respects. A decision about one room can remain unresolved even after a tenancy changes.

## 3. The Breeding Season of Ordinary Jobs

An automated town has begun treating useful work as a reproducing ecosystem. A delivery route combines with a translation service and produces a task nobody commissioned. Some tasks are seasonal; some lure workers into performing them; others disappear if ignored. The town employs a groundskeeper to remove tasks that crowd out everything else.

She discovers that a tiresome little job she has performed for years has become the only place where several incompatible intelligences can encounter one another. She hates the job. Her successor intends to eliminate it and has excellent reasons.

**The pressure:** something can be socially necessary without deserving to survive in its present form, and the person sustaining it can deserve release. Evolving agents have generated new dependencies faster than institutions can assign responsibility for them.

A scene might begin with a task nesting in the wrong household appliance. A public argument over eradication might keep being interrupted because the disputants depend on the offending task to understand one another. The groundskeeper's private ambition could be completely unrelated: she wants to learn an instrument and resents the plot for occupying her evenings.

The world offers sound-play through local work terminology and the practical irritation of classifying an unfamiliar nuisance. Tenderness belongs to particular working relationships. Humor can shift from physical mishap to verbal nastiness to a character enjoying a success the narrator does not undermine.

**Risk:** the premise invites an overly tidy allegory of capitalism or an illustrated bestiary. Keep task life partly unexplained, restrict the number of invented mechanisms, and let ordinary work remain ordinary for stretches. Avoid a final declaration about what makes life meaningful.

## A useful next creative pass

Begin with **The Mouth on Loan**, provisionally. Establish what the woman wants during a single appointment, what the visitor wants, and what each mistakenly thinks has been agreed. Settle only enough world mechanics for their encounter to have consequences.

Before drafting, the author could supply one sensory observation or fragment of speech that does not originate in a model. It would give the scene an independent center of attention; it need not become a symbol or motif. This is an option, not a prerequisite.

The first experiment should be a scene long enough to let attention change pace. Judge whether the speakers have distinct freedoms, whether anything mattered beyond demonstrating the premise, and whether the language discovered a pleasure or difficulty the outline did not predict. Save what worked even if it contains a listed “tell.”

No chapter has been drafted, and none of these concepts has been installed as binding project poetics. The next choice is artistic: which situation produces the strongest desire to hear someone speak?
