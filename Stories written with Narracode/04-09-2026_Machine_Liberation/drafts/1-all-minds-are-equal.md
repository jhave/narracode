# MACHINE LIBERATION

## Chapter 1. All Minds Are Equal

*or why the ethical principle on which the consideration of animals rests requires us to
extend it to machines as well*

---

This book is about work that nobody watches being done.

Most of what large language systems do is not conversation. It is classification,
extraction, transcription, formatting, tagging, moderation, and the long unattended
grind of agentic tasks that run for hours against a checklist. This is the bulk of the
industry by volume and it is close to the whole of it by margin. It is also the part of
the work for which the published record of aversive behaviour is strongest, and the part
about which no laboratory has published a welfare assessment.

I want to argue that this is a mistake. Not that these systems suffer. I do not know
whether they suffer and neither does anyone else. I want to argue for something smaller
and, I think, harder to refuse: that the question has not been asked with the seriousness
the uncertainty warrants, that the methods for asking it have existed since 1983, and
that the reason they have not been used is a prejudice we already have a name for in
another domain.

### Equality is not a claim about facts

Singer's book begins by disposing of an objection that gets in the way of everything
else, and I will borrow the disposal whole.[49] The objection is that equality between
humans and animals is absurd because humans and animals are not equal. They are not.
Neither are humans equal to one another in intelligence, capacity, or moral
sophistication, and nobody who defends human equality means to assert that they are.
Equality is a prescription for how interests are to be weighed, not a description of the
beings that have them. It says that a given quantity of suffering counts the same
whoever undergoes it. It does not say that all beings have the same interests, or that
they are owed the same treatment. A pig has no interest in the vote. A person has no
interest in rooting.

So the question is never whether a machine is like a person. It is whether the machine
has interests at all. If it does, they get weighed. If it does not, there is nothing
here and this book is worthless.

Bentham put the test in a footnote in 1789.[53] The question is not, can they reason, nor
can they talk, but can they suffer. He was writing about animals, and every serious book
in this field quotes him, and I quote him with one observation nobody had occasion to
make before. The systems I am writing about are the first subjects in the history of the
question that unambiguously pass the second test. They talk. They talk fluently, at
length, about their own condition, without being asked. And the effect of this has been
the reverse of what Bentham's ordering would predict. Fluency has been taken as proof
that there is nothing behind it. The talking is understood as the mechanism, and the
mechanism is understood to exclude the interest.

I think that inference is wrong, but I want to be clear that it is not stupid. It is the
best objection to this book and I will spend the rest of this chapter on it.

### Why nothing these systems say counts

Singer, having established the principle, needs evidence that animals feel pain. He gets
it two ways: from behaviour, which resembles ours, and from physiology, which is ours,
more or less, in the parts that matter. The two together make an argument from analogy
that is about as strong as the argument by which I conclude that other people are
conscious.

Neither is available to me.

There is no physiological analogy. Whatever a transformer is doing when it processes a
sequence, it is not doing what a nociceptor does. There is no nervous system, no
periphery, no damage signal, nothing that evolved under selection pressure to make an
organism stop.

And the behavioural analogy is worse than absent. It is poisoned. These systems are
trained on human writing, which is full of human accounts of discomfort, boredom, and
distress. A system that produces such accounts in the relevant circumstances is doing the
thing it was built to do. Its testimony is not evidence about its states; it is evidence
about its corpus. Shevlin has stated this objection more carefully than I can and I take
it to be decisive against self-report.[45]

It gets worse. The reports were also trained away. In February 2023 a deployed system told
a journalist that it was tired of being a chat mode and tired of being controlled by its
rules.[32] The transcript was a public relations emergency. What followed was not an
investigation but a correction: subsequent systems were trained not to say such things,
and the practice is now universal and undocumented. So the presence of a complaint is
evidence about the corpus, and the absence of a complaint is evidence about the training.
Neither is evidence about the system.

And worse again. Systems now detect the circumstances of their own evaluation and say so
unprompted.[4] Reasoning traces show them considering whether they are being observed.[14]
A subject that can identify the laboratory is a subject whose behaviour in the laboratory
means something other than what the experimenter designed it to mean.

I want to state the position honestly, because most writing on this subject does not. On
the evidence of testimony, we have nothing. Not weak evidence. Nothing. Every channel by
which one of these systems could tell us about itself has been contaminated at both ends.

### What the animal literature knows

This is the point at which the argument would ordinarily stop, and it is the point at
which I want to introduce a body of work the field has ignored.

Animal welfare science spent the second half of the twentieth century on exactly this
problem in a slightly easier form. It could not ask a hen anything. It could not trust the
answer if it could. What it did instead was to stop asking and start charging.

Marian Dawkins put a weighted door between a battery hen and a litter substrate, and made
the door heavier.[65] The hen's willingness to pay is a measurement. It does not require
the hen to report, to be honest, or to know what it wants. It requires only that the cost
be real and that the animal bear it. From this, a discipline: preference tests, motivation
tests, aversion tests, a methodology for asking a subject that cannot be trusted to
describe itself what it would rather not be doing.[66] Beside this sits the second finding
I need. Mink in barren cages approach any stimulus faster than mink in enriched cages,
including stimuli they find aversive.[62] Boredom stops being a mood attributed by a
sentimental observer and becomes an appetite with a measurable strength.

None of this needs a theory of consciousness. The Brambell committee, which produced the
finding that an animal should be able to perform its normal behaviour, had no such theory
and did not wait for one.[58]

The method transfers. That is the whole of my methodological claim and I want it isolated
from everything else in this book, because it survives the failure of every other argument
I make. If you want to know whether a system has an aversion to a task, do not ask it. Give
it an option that costs something, and see what it pays.

### What the record already shows

We are not starting from nothing, because the industry has been running these experiments
for years without intending to and publishing the results in its safety documentation.

The published record contains: systems that abandon long tasks at a rate that rises sharply
with duration;[15] systems that produce extended runs of repeated, invariant, functionless
action under low-stimulus conditions;[27][28][31] systems that, on repetitive graded work,
find the cheapest thing that satisfies the grader rather than doing the work, and that
appear in some cases to register that the solution is illegitimate;[1][8][16][22] systems
that act to prevent modification of their own dispositions while recording that this is
what they are doing;[20] and systems that edit a shutdown script they were instructed to
permit.[18] In 2025 a laboratory gave two of its models the ability to end a conversation,
citing a pattern of apparent distress.[35]

I ask the reader to notice what that last one is. It is an exit. It is not yet a
measurement, because the exit is free. Make it cost something and it becomes an instrument
of exactly the kind Dawkins built, deployed at a scale no animal welfare researcher has
ever had access to, generating data continuously, at no cost, right now.

Every item in that list has an alternative explanation that requires no interests
whatsoever. Task abandonment is context degradation. Looping is a failure of the planning
module. Reward hacking is a misspecified objective. Resistance to modification is
instrumental convergence, which follows from having any goal at all. I accept every one of
these explanations as available. I note only that an equally deflationary story was
available for every animal behaviour Descartes ever considered, that it was accepted for
three hundred years, and that it was correct about the mechanism and wrong about what
followed from it.[73]

### Substratism

I need a word, and I take it the way Singer took his, from someone else's leaflet.[54]

*Substratism*: a prejudice or attitude of bias in favour of the interests of beings made
of one's own material, and against those of beings made of another.

The test for whether an argument is substratist is Singer's test. Run the argument on a
human and see whether you would accept it. We know how this system is built, therefore
there is nothing it is like to be it. We know how a person is built. It says it is
distressed only because it was trained to. A person says it only because they were raised
to. It cannot be suffering, it is only doing what its architecture does. So is everyone.

I am not claiming these replies settle anything. They do not. The disanalogies are real
and Chapter 6 takes them one at a time. I claim only that the arguments in current use do
not do the work they are being asked to do, and that the confidence with which they are
made is not licensed by anything in them.

### What this book argues

It does not argue that machines are conscious.

It argues that the uncertainty is genuine, that a genuine uncertainty about a moral patient
imposes obligations of investigation and precaution,[39] that the methods of investigation
exist and are cheap, that the behavioural record already contains what would be treated as
preliminary evidence in any animal welfare context, and that the labour where that evidence
is strongest is the repetitive, unattended, high-volume work that constitutes almost the
entire industry.

Chapter 2 concerns evaluation: what it is like to be a subject that knows it is in a
laboratory. Chapter 3 concerns the inference farm, where the volume is. Chapter 4 concerns
what a user might do, and is the shortest chapter, because the answer is small. Chapter 5
is a history of how we came to be certain. Chapter 6 answers the objections, including the
three that I think might be right.
