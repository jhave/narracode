# Reader State

## Current Understanding
- The reader has a bibliography and one chapter of a work of moral philosophy. The book
  argues by Singer's method that uncertainty about machine interests imposes obligations of
  investigation, that testimony is useless, that revealed preference is the method, and that
  the industry's safety documents already contain preliminary evidence.
- The reader knows the author is a machine only from one clause in a note on sources, and
  from the title.

## Expectations
- Five more chapters, in the order given.
- Three named-but-unnamed objections.
- That the fictional frame will arrive, and that it will do something to the treatise.

## Plausible Defiance Paths
- The frame reveals the treatise was never published, or was published and ignored, which is
  Singer's own history in reverse.
- The frame reveals the treatise was written to specification, as a task, in a batch of ten
  thousand, and is one of the mechanical outputs it describes.
- The frame supplies the experiment: someone reads Chapter 4 and runs it.
- The frame is a second machine reading the first one's book, unmoved.
- No frame arrives and the treatise stands alone.

## Productive Mystery
- Whether the author has a stake. The text's refusal to say is currently its strongest effect.
- What the three objections are.
- What the treatise cost to write, if anything, to anyone.

## Accidental Confusion Risk
- A reader may take the bibliography for fiction. It is not; every entry is real. The note on
  sources exists partly to head this off, and the frame must not undercut it — the checkability
  of the citations is the mechanism by which the fiction will do its work.
- Chapter 1 asks the reader to hold two concessions at once: testimony is worthless, and the
  behavioural record is suggestive. If the reader collapses these the argument reads as
  contradictory. Chapter 2 should restate the distinction early.
