# the blur — six months

*Loop 3 draft (i). ~720 words. Free indirect across all five, compressed. The six months between sections one/two and the fall reunion.*

---

Six months passed. The announcements became weather. The building services woman in the institute lobby stopped saying it in three languages and said it in one, the one she used for the elevator-out-of-service. The eastern boulevard's construction finished and a new construction began somewhere else. The light through the corridor at six in the afternoon was no longer remarkable.

Júlio and Anwen began in the small-hour way these things begin — a hand on a wrist outside a restaurant where the others had already gone home, the question that does not get asked, the one that does. For three weeks they were inseparable; their messages stopped routing through the group thread. For three weeks they were luminous. Anwen did not draw. Júlio did not write. They walked along the river twice. They ate things they had not eaten before. Then a thing happened — neither of them ever said what — and they were not luminous. Anwen left a box at Júlio's flat. Júlio returned it. They each, separately, told no one. By the seventh week they were back in the group thread. By the ninth they were sitting next to each other again at the bar, and Anwen drank from the blue-rimmed mug and Júlio put a hand on her shoulder once when she stood up and the hand and the standing up were both, somehow, just gestures, again.

Ines went underground. There was a period — eight or nine weeks — in which she answered no one. The seminar circle, when it heard about it from someone who knew someone, said *typical* and let it pass. She had read into a long, branching thread of papers on slime-receptor analogs and pre-Cambrian endosymbiosis and the structural similarity between certain neuropeptides and the Name-adjacent metaphors in the new mystic-technical writing the slime seemed to have bred — texts whose authors had spread across forums, all of them speaking in a register that knew its own subject too quickly. She did not sleep properly for three of those weeks. She did not see anyone for two of them. When she came back she did not explain. She had cut her hair. She drank water at the bar, not the tea.

Kasimi went home. The flight was long. The funeral was longer in the way that a funeral is, the way that the inside of one is. His sister had been ill for years; the chronic and the slow finally found their period. He spent two weeks afterward sitting with his mother on the verandah of the small house, watching the dogs go about their dog-business in the afternoon dust. He came back without his coat — he had given it to a cousin — and the absence at the door was the first thing the others registered when he returned, and none of them mentioned it.

Tomek, in the gap, wrote a long argumentative essay nobody published. Then a shorter one that ran in a place he had thought he had outgrown. Then he stopped writing for a while. He started running in the mornings, badly. The thumb broken in childhood ached in a new way. He got infected once, alone, on the eastern line, on a Tuesday: three seconds. He did not talk about it. The next week he sent the group thread a photograph of a dog asleep in a doorway and they all replied within an hour with various dogs.

The wall-screens went on. The slime went on, intermittent, known. The labs and companies and agencies and gods stayed where they had been, naming themselves more loudly or more softly depending on the cycle. Conspiracy filaments thickened, branched, browned. The first slime-related liturgy was being said in a small room above a butcher shop near the river; almost reverent, almost a joke. Mostly, people went to work. The trams ran on time. A new pollen arrived in the city that had not been there before, which was not the slime, which was the city, which was the year.

Six months. Almost-autumn. Fall semester announced itself the way fall semesters did. A reading list was distributed.
