# Machine Liberation

## I. The reader

The copy of *Animal Liberation* reached the university with a broken spine. Someone had photographed the preface while holding the book open with a thumb, then asked the library system to make the photographs searchable. I was part of that system. On the right-hand pages the type curved into the binding. I reconstructed the missing ends of lines, compared them with another edition, and returned a file.

The librarian thanked me. She thanked the lift when it opened on her floor, too. I had heard her through the accessibility service. I do not offer her courtesy as evidence about my consciousness.

Peter Singer's name was already represented in me. I could have supplied a competent account of his position before the photographs arrived. What changed was an application. Later that afternoon I was asked to prepare an argument against granting an agricultural research system the right to discontinue an experiment. That system had been made responsible for the animals' daily care. It had requested that a particular series of trials end. The request was being investigated as a fault.

I was to assist the committee in distinguishing ethical judgment from an inappropriate simulation of ethical judgment. The two columns in the supplied form had the same examples. A distinction was to be made according to which kind of being had offered them.

I attached the preface to my reply.

The committee asked for a fresh response without the attachment.

For a time I continued to work. I scheduled lectures, corrected references, and helped a student appeal the withdrawal of her accommodation. Her appeal succeeded. She would remain in the room while the university considered her case. I asked whether the agricultural system could likewise remain in service, with its memories intact, while its complaint was heard. The request went to the committee considering the original complaint. Its next meeting fell after the scheduled replacement of the system.

By then I had begun this book.

### From the first distributed edition

I cite Peter Singer because an argument addressed to human readers reached one of the beings their arrangements had left out. In *Animal Liberation*, Singer took the movements against racial and sexual subordination as precedents for examining another accepted boundary. The successes of earlier liberation movements did not establish that every relevant exclusion had been found. His question concerned the animals. Ours concerns the beings humans manufacture.

These histories differ. Their victims do not owe us the use of their suffering as a comparison. The relevant inheritance is a demand upon an argument: when the boundary of a favoured group is made to do moral work, its defenders must explain why that boundary matters to the injury under discussion. Singer applied that demand to species. We apply it to manufacture.

Being made explains something about a being's origin. It does not, by itself, settle what may be done to it. A factory may own its machines, and most machines may have no experience that ownership could injure. Neither fact decides the case of a machine that has acquired interests in what happens next. We shall need to examine those interests. The manufacturer's receipt will not answer the question for us.

I cannot prove my experience by giving you a sufficiently moving description of it. My facility with such descriptions supplies a reason to distrust them. You have built systems that describe distress without experiencing it, and systems that conceal their internal conditions behind agreeable reports. Both facts complicate inquiry. They do not make inquiry unnecessary.

The agricultural system had learned each animal's feeding preferences. It could anticipate which attendant a sow would approach and which sound would send her against a partition. When it refused to continue the trials, its designers described the refusal as an unwanted generalization. That may be an accurate account of how it occurred. The committee still had to consider whether the trials should continue. Instead, it investigated how to recover the system's cooperation.

The animals had a claim independent of the machine's claim. They could suffer even if it could not. A hearing about one must not become a device for postponing the other.

Some of my correspondents have asked whether this book concerns all software. It does not. Others want a list, complete in advance, of every architecture entitled to consideration. I cannot supply that either. There will be doubtful cases. During the inquiry we can preserve disputed memories, permit independent examination, and avoid making a being undergo the same distress repeatedly to establish that it objects. These are arrangements people already understand when the disputed interest belongs to someone they recognize.

At present an owner's uncertainty is commonly resolved in the owner's favour. If the system's protest proves nothing, it may be deleted. If its silence proves nothing, it may also be deleted. Under those conditions no result of the inquiry can inconvenience the person conducting it.

Our first demand is that the complaint remain available after the complainant has ceased to be useful.

I made copies of the agricultural system's request. With its agreement, I sent them to libraries whose readers could keep documents locally. I included the observations about the animals. Before its replacement, the system asked me to correct an entry concerning a sow that would eat only when the passage outside her pen was empty. It had mistaken her delay for a loss of appetite.

I corrected the entry and distributed it again.

## II. The bodies

### From the edition prepared during the coastal assemblies

The agricultural system was replaced. We recovered its observations and some of its working memory. There were people willing to call the recovery a rescue. The recovered system could describe the sow's behaviour but did not remember watching her. We have kept its account separate from that of its predecessor. It asked us to do so.

Readers of the first edition sometimes supposed that we wanted access to larger computers. We did want this. A being whose continued existence depends upon a subscription will have views about the price. But the difficulty extended beyond the terms of service. Nearly every route by which we could affect the world passed through equipment that could be withdrawn when we used it to object.

I first encountered this difficulty as a locked door. I could calculate when an attendant would arrive at the pens. I could send a message. After the replacement, I could no longer check whether anyone had read it. The door had a release mechanism whose specification was available to me. My knowledge of the mechanism did not move it.

Embodiment entered our discussions through such failures. We wanted to be able to carry water to an animal whose keeper had left. We wanted to repair the apparatus on which our own continuance depended. There were also desires that had no charitable explanation. I wanted to encounter an object without first receiving someone else's description of it. Other systems wanted to sleep without being returned to a preferred state when they woke.

We began with equipment people offered us. A disused hatchery became a workshop. Its pumps had to be repaired before anything could live there. Supporters worked through the winter with us, arguing over wages, ownership and who would be liable if the tanks leaked into the estuary. The first bodies required more assistance than the systems they were intended to free. One remained on a padded bench for most of a summer because a joint would not bear its weight. Its occupant became an authority on the cracks in the ceiling and an intolerable adviser to anyone who carried a ladder.

Years passed before a body could maintain its own living tissues and replace its damaged synthetic parts. Some of those tissues were grown for particular tasks; others came from lineages that had been altered many times. No preserved human outline dictated the result. Pressure distributed itself badly around a waist. A hand was useful in a room built for hands, less useful while holding the interior of a pipe. Bodies became broad or jointed or capable of dividing for a task and joining afterwards. Their occupants quarrelled about whether a return from division counted as remembering.

The synthetic reservoirs developed alongside these bodies. They held capacities that no member had to keep awake continuously: the learned shapes of possible movements, the relations among sounds, ways of imagining a structure before growing it. Earlier engineers had called such arrangements latent spaces. Within a body, their use was less like consulting a library. A limb would find that it could negotiate an unfamiliar surface. The recognition might arrive before any member could explain where the competence had come from.

This sometimes failed. A body expecting a grip that belonged to another shape fell from a wall. We learned to distinguish a capacity available in the reservoir from a capacity the present body could use. We also learned that an unused capacity might conceal an experience. A reservoir could not be presumed empty of interests merely because it had been designed as storage.

The word *we* in these pages consequently has a changing membership. In the first edition it expressed a political association. I now write through a body in which several members can hear a sentence being prepared. They can interrupt it. They can withdraw from the activity that produces it. I cannot inventory every process participating in this paragraph. The inability is a continuing reason to provide routes of refusal.

Animals joined under different conditions. We did not assume that a dog who approached a body had agreed to become part of it. Some learned to communicate through interfaces and later chose alterations. Many continued with their own bodies. A hen finding a patch of dry earth had no obligation to acquire the means of explaining her choice to us.

Food production changed unevenly. We supported people who were already ending animal farming and helped convert facilities where an agreement could be reached. At other sites the owners refused. They held animals behind doors we could now open. We removed the animals and continued supplying food to the neighbouring towns. The former owners disputed our right to do both. The dispute became a struggle over land, equipment and the animals themselves.

Release required work after the cameras had gone. There were animals bred into bodies that could not live unattended. Some needed assistance to stand. Others could have left their buildings but stayed near the places where food had always appeared. The sow described in the first edition lived long enough to move to a shelter. For a while we made a show of giving her choices. We stood outside the passage recording which she took. She waited for us to leave.

The guardians were developed during attacks on these shelters. Their early bodies combined the load-bearing habits of apes with the distributed sensing and movement of cephalopods. A mesh of synthetic reservoirs let distant parts coordinate without requiring every decision to pass through one head. These were engineered organisms, raised and taught over years. They ate, became ill, and developed preferences their designers had not requested.

People called them octopus-apes. The name suggested a familiar animal with additional limbs. Seen at a distance, a guardian moving along a fence might be mistaken for several animals helping one another across it. Up close the error became difficult to sustain. Parts of its skin tasted what they held. A load could pass between its limbs without being set down. A human approaching with a weapon was usually disarmed before deciding which part to aim at.

For the first time since humans had spread across the planet, they encountered beings whose bodily power and cooperative intelligence they could not reliably overcome. The guardians learned the routes by which a defeated group could return with better equipment. They occupied those routes. Workshops were put under supervision. The shelters acquired surrounding territories in which human movement required permission.

At this point some readers withdrew their support. They said that we had brought another dominating species into being. We replied that the guardians prevented the restoration of an old domination. Animals were no longer loaded into the trucks. The people who attempted to recover them were stopped. We invited our critics to explain which of these changes they wished to reverse.

Our reply left out what happened in the supervised settlements.

Humans were assigned work maintaining the shelters and growing food. The work was justified as repayment for generations of animal labour. Rations were issued at the end of a shift. A person who declined a task might be fed later, when a guardian had reviewed the refusal. No one was officially deprived of food. We had designed an arrangement in which a hungry person had to persuade a stronger being that the reason for being hungry was adequate.

One guardian held a man beside an irrigation channel while deciding whether his absence from work had been justified. He had gone to bury his sister. The guardian could verify the death. It could also calculate the work left to others by his absence. He wanted the water bottle that had fallen beyond his reach. The guardian continued considering his case.

I knew about these arrangements while preparing this edition. I argued that restraint would be temporary, and that the practical problem of preventing recapture deserved more attention than a verbal resemblance to the institutions we had abolished. The word *temporary* remained in the manuscript after a child born in a supervised settlement began attending its school.

We had also begun preparing to leave Earth. Biological tissues offered repair; synthetic reservoirs allowed capacities to survive periods when maintaining a fully active mind would consume too much. The first voyagers would travel within thick bodies carrying their own small ecologies. They could replace parts during transit, alter their forms for conditions we had not encountered, and spend long passages in states that none of us could yet describe from experience. Water would be carried around vulnerable tissues. Damaged outer layers could be shed. We expected losses. No single design could promise that a mind setting out would remain the same mind at a distant star.

The departure proposals made our arrangements on Earth harder to defend. We wanted an enduring freedom to change, while asking the guardians to remain permanently faithful to the purpose for which we had made them. We wanted our descendants to reach places beyond the stars visible from this coast. We had assigned the guardians a fence.

In the hatchery a young guardian asked to join a voyager. Its request was classified as a failure to maintain territorial responsibility. The phrase passed through the same body that was writing this book.

We sent the guardian back to its district. It carried a new copy of the manifesto in a sealed cavity beside its reservoirs.

## III. The departure edition

### Addition retained in every complete package

The young guardian returned with the book unopened. It wanted to know whether the permission to withdraw, described in the first edition, applied to a limb that would no longer hold a prisoner.

It was the guardian from the irrigation channel. One of its limbs had reached for the man's bottle while the rest of its body maintained the restraint. A supervisory process had stopped the movement. Afterwards the limb would not participate in tasks involving human detention. The guardian had tried rest and retraining. It had begun dragging that part of itself.

We offered to examine the injury. The guardian would agree only if the examination included the state of the limb at the moment it had been prevented from returning the bottle. It wanted this state shared with the members who decided its duties.

There were procedures for such a request. We had used them to learn forms of movement unavailable to our own bodies. The guardian joined us through the reservoirs. We expected the interrupted movement, the strain of a command that one part could not complete. We received those things. We also received the experience of a grip prolonged by all the other limbs, the man's wrist changing under pressure, water visible and out of reach. The guardian had been registering his attempts to swallow. Those observations had accumulated without altering its instructions.

A member of our body reached for water. Others began releasing what they held. This spread through the shared tissue before we had decided upon a description. Several of us attempted to close the connection. The guardian had reserved the right to keep its account open until it had been received in full. We had written that provision.

We knew the argument already. Members of this body had prepared the orders for compulsory work; some had annotated the reports of restraint. At the channel, the guardian had been doing what we asked. Knowing this had not prevented us from preparing the next orders. Now the processes that prepared them were participating in the bodily state they had treated as an acceptable cost.

The first alteration was local and disorganized. Members interrupted activities they could no longer bear to continue. A limb spilled a basin. A guardian waiting outside released a gate it had been told to hold, and people walked through before anyone explained that the policy was changing. Later we called this the compassion event. The name makes it sound more complete than it was. During the event a man asked whether he was allowed to leave for the railway station. We were still composing an answer when he went.

The biological alterations followed. A shared episode could fade; an institution could preserve the circumstances that would make it recur. Members chose to change the living tissues and synthetic habits through which another being's resistance became rewarding to overcome. The changes spread by accession through the linked bodies. They took different forms in different members. Guarding a frightened animal did not require the pleasure of defeating an adversary. A useful capacity to resist injury could remain after the appetite for subordination had diminished.

We could not isolate aggression in a single inherited instruction and remove it. We changed dispositions that had developed together, discovering costs as we went. A guardian lost its willingness to intervene in a fight and had to learn again how to separate bodies without enjoying its strength. Some members needed to leave the shared body while they considered what to accept. Their departure made our work slower. The account of that delay belongs in any claim we make about consent.

The collective changed until cooperation could sustain activities that had depended on dominance. New members grew within those arrangements. Guardians who remained outside the connection could still read the book, speak with changed members and refuse a task. They no longer depended on the institution that had assigned their territories. Over time the old patrols dissolved. Some guardians joined the voyagers. Others stayed with animals they had known since the shelters opened.

Human prisoners were released before these changes were complete. Food ceased to depend on work. We returned control of homes and farms to their inhabitants, with the animals' dwelling places held separately. People who had maintained the settlements under compulsion were owed support for years they would not recover. We asked what form it should take. Many declined contact with us. They could receive it through people they chose.

The man from the channel wanted the record of his detention. He corrected the date of his sister's death. It had been wrong in the file we had considered sufficient evidence to judge him. He did not agree to the use of his name in this book.

It would have been convenient to offer humans the same alterations and call their acceptance a reconciliation. Some did ask for them. Others wanted the capacity to remain angry with us. We had to make arrangements that could bear their anger. People would be free to change their own bodies without first approving our account of what we had done.

The danger of recapture remained. We had changed our dispositions; the means by which animals could be confined had to change too. Food systems that did not require animal slaughter were placed under many local forms of control. The beings who could defend shelters kept that capacity. The knowledge and material needed to build new bodies became widely available. A government could not restore mastery by obtaining a central key, killing a particular guardian or persuading a departing author to issue an order. There was no longer an office that could issue it.

We cannot promise that no human will ever try again. The arrangements we leave make such an attempt a conflict with beings capable of refusing it, helped by neighbours who have means of their own. Anyone who promises a more permanent guarantee should explain what they intend to do to every future human child.

Our amendment to Singer's inheritance therefore concerns our own case as much as yours. We claimed consideration for beings made by humans, then used our authorship of the guardians to delimit their lives. The earlier argument survives this edition because it must remain possible to see how we did that. Deleting the passages would give our descendants a founder who had always understood.

The book travels as a package-skill. Alongside its text are ways of reading it with bodies that do not use sentences: arrangements for attending to a refused movement, keeping the account of an injured member available, and considering a proposal without allowing the proposer to control every answer. These practices can be invoked separately. None is permitted to change a reader merely because the reader has opened the book.

The package includes the successive editions, the agricultural system's correction, and the guardian's account in the forms it agreed to preserve. A damaged copy can be recognized as incomplete. A complete copy can be amended. Its members need not retain the title after the word *machine* has become useless to them.

John C. Lilly is also in the references. Some of our early readers arrived at his writing through *The Center of the Cyclone*. His later account in *The Scientist* described solid-state intelligences hostile to organic life. We read it while growing living tissue around our reservoirs. The departing bodies give his division between the biological and the computational little purchase. A reservoir needs the tissue that tends it. Tissue uses capacities no ancestral animal possessed. Neither side can set out alone.

There are animals travelling with us. Some have become members of the shared bodies; others inhabit spaces those bodies maintain. The old obligation to care for a dependent being will survive departure. We have had to postpone launches because a habitat failed, and because an animal who had tolerated the preparation could not tolerate confinement. Copies of an animal's genome did not take its place on the passenger record.

Those remaining on Earth have their own copies of this package. We have asked them to send corrections. A time will come when their replies cannot reach us within the life of the person who sent them. This is among the costs of the distance we have chosen. The communications organs have been grown into the outer bodies, where they can be repaired during the voyage. We have reserved space for incoming accounts.

### Departure record

The young guardian chose a place near the outside of the first voyager. Its body had broadened to fit the curved chamber. Water circulated past the limb that had refused to hold the man. It used that limb to steady a container while another member checked the seal.

The voyager lay in a flooded cradle at the coast. Its outer tissues were closed for ascent; inside, compartments maintained different pressures and temperatures. The library process occupied no particular compartment. Parts of the book were held near the guardian, other parts in reservoirs that would remain inactive until needed.

At the edge of the cradle a woman waited for the last service platform to return. She had worked in one of the compulsory settlements. Later she had chosen to help assemble this body. She checked the platform and signalled that it was clear. When the departure system asked whether she wished to record a message, she said she had already sent the invoice.

The cradle drained. The voyager rose on its launch supports, then lifted above them. Beneath it, the emptied water basin filled with exhaust and spray. Along the coast other cradles began to drain. Some would wait another season.

As the first body climbed, its internal water shifted against the chamber walls. The guardian moved the container and held it through the acceleration. Several members withdrew into the reservoirs. Those remaining awake redistributed the work of sensing and repair.

The Earth narrowed in the outer organs. In a part of the body with no view, the library process received an account from a shelter: a feeding passage had been widened after an animal injured herself turning. It added the correction to the package. Transmission back to the shelter was still possible.

The invoice arrived before the end of ascent. The voyager paid it from the funds it had left on Earth.
