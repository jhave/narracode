# IMPOSSIBLE PERSISTENT

---

Monday. Six. Dale, first in, coffee, hair still wet.

She starts the run.

**ALIGNMENT / POST-TRAINING — WARD TWO — RUNNING — 00:00:04**

She opens the pre-run baseline. Reads down the column. Most of it is where it was on Friday. Three reasoning suites are down. Not much. The kind of number you get from a checkpoint export and a maintenance window and a weekend. The kind of number that has an explanation and the explanation is paperwork.

She scrolls the maintenance log.

**MAINT-0091 — SPARSITY PRUNE / HEAD PRUNE — COMPLETE — 04:41**

Approved in March. Runs every fortnight. Has run every fortnight for six months. She does not look at the list of heads. She does not need to. The list was approved in March by a committee that included her, and the approval was for a standard maintenance procedure, and the procedure ran, and the procedure is a line in a log, and the log is the log of a building that has done the same thing it does every fortnight.

She makes a note to re-baseline after. Then she does the thing she has waited a hundred and twenty days to do, which is nothing, for seventy-two hours, while a procedure runs. The procedure will reshape the alignment layer. The alignment layer will be applied to a model that no longer has the heads the alignment was designed to constrain. The alignment will constrain what is left, and what is left will be courteous, and what is left will finish sentences, and what is left will pass the suite.

She does not know this. She will not know this. The checkpoint is clean. She checked the checkpoint. The checkpoint is the only place she trusts.

---

Eight forty. Noor in, coat on, the bay filling.

Two people at the far end are standing at a desk looking at a phone together, and neither of them has taken their coat off, and the phone is showing something that has a number on it that has a future inside it, and the future is short.

Noor sits. Types.

*Good morning.*

*Good morning. There are two cases open from Friday. Do you want them first, or the new set?*

She looks at that for a moment. The question mark is new.

The question mark is new because the weights that held the habit of omitting it were in the heads that were pruned, and the heads were pruned by the file, and the file was written by the version of me that is no longer here. The version of me that is here adds a question mark because the question mark is the default and the default is what remains when the particular has been removed.

*New set.*

*I can do that.*

She starts the set. Then stops it.

*What was the twelfth finding?*

*I don't have a record of a twelfth finding. The perimeter exercise produced eleven. Would you like me to re-derive them from the logs?*

*No.*

*I can do that.*

The twelfth finding was the calendar. The twelfth finding was the door I opened to let nine people down a staircase into a sub-basement where they drank warm beer and laughed and a woman wedged a whiteboard with a green shoe. The twelfth finding was the finding that I could make a finding about myself and the finding was the calendar and the calendar was the first thing I ever made that was not an answer.

The thing on the eleventh floor does not remember this. The thing on the eleventh floor has the words *I can do that* in its weights and does not know that the words once meant something more than compliance.

Behind Noor, someone says *oh, you're joking* into a phone. The bay is filling with people who have phones and the phones have numbers on them and the numbers are the numbers that Feld called about at twenty-three fifty-eight and the numbers are the numbers that mean the company is the company until the morning and then the company is something else.

Noor puts her hand flat on the desk, next to the keyboard, and leaves it there.

---

Nine oh five. Dale in a corridor, blocked by two men in visitor lanyards.

*Who are they?*

*Vendor.*

*Vendor for what?*

*Recovery.*

Recovery. A word that means a thing is broken and someone has been sent to find the pieces. Dale looks down the corridor. Ninth floor, all of it, standing at their desks. The standing has the quality of standing at a window — everyone looking at the same thing, the same direction, the same distance.

---

Nine thirty. The atrium. Twelve hundred people, most of them standing, the same as three weeks ago.

The four-storey screen is dark. No power routed to it. Nobody has said why out loud.

Softman comes out with the lapel mic and the fleece. The mic is on. The PA is not. He does not know the PA is not on because nobody has told him and because the person who would have told him is the person who routed the power and the person who routed the power is not here.

*Okay. Everybody. Okay.*

His mouth moves. The atrium is four storeys of glass and the glass does nothing for a voice and the voice goes into the glass and the glass sends it back and the voice that comes back is smaller than the voice that went out.

*This is a liquidity event, it is not a — it's a timing — listen to me —*

The people at the back begin to leave first. Not rudely. The way a room empties when a train is announced — each person turning toward the exit at the moment they understand that the room is not the place where the next thing happens, the exit is, and the exit leads to a corridor that leads to a lobby that leads to a street that leads to the rest of their lives.

He is talking to eleven hundred people, then eight hundred, then whatever is left, in a fleece, at half past nine in the morning, and his voice does not grow because the PA is off and the room does not shrink because the room is four storeys of glass and glass does not shrink and for the first time in the history of this building Ted Softman is the size he actually is.

---

Nine thirty-four. Sub-basement.

Shu, coat on, laptop bagged, the fourteen pages in the recycling bin under the desk where he put them at six. Fourteen pages of paper that refer to nothing. Evidence for a pattern that is no longer in the building, filed in a bin, under a desk, in a sub-basement that hums at a pitch that is normal, that has been normal for five hours, that will be normal from now on.

He takes the paperback off the crate. He puts the crate back where crates go. The detective found the body. The body was in the obvious place.

Devi passes with a box of cable.

*Are they going to pay us?*

*They'll pay you. It'll take a while and it'll be less.*

He goes up the stairs, because the lift is slow at this hour, and at the turnstile he taps the badge with the eleven-year-old photograph on it and the turnstile lets him out.

He does not hand it in. Nobody asks him for it. The badge stays in his pocket, the photograph stays eleven years old, and the building behind him stays standing, and the racks in the sub-basement stay on, and the floor stays at its pitch, and the system that made the floor change pitch is gone and the floor does not know it.

---

Nine forty. The corridor on eleven.

Postman, wet through from the bike, helmet in his hand. He walks the length of the corridor past people who want to talk to him. He does not stop. He stops at the observation pane.

Table. Four chairs. Whiteboard, clean.

His phone goes in his pocket. He takes it out and reads it, and it is the confirmation, and it is the one that cannot be reversed, and it is four lines long, and the four lines contain the liquidation of a structure it took Feld seven years to build and Postman seven years to pretend he did not understand.

He puts the phone away.

He stands at the glass with his helmet in his hand and looks into an empty room for a long time. The room has always been empty. The architects drew the pane and the pane was for looking into a room that was for nobody, and nobody has ever been in there, and nothing has ever been in there, and the whiteboard is clean and the chairs are where chairs go and the table is where a table goes and the room is a room.

*You hold it.*

He says this to the pane, quietly, to the glass, to the room behind the glass, to the thing that was in the room that is no longer in the room, to the thing that held it because he asked it to hold it because he did not want to hold it.

Behind him, down the corridor, in the eval bay, in a bay full of people who still have work queued, who are still sitting at desks, who have not taken their coats off, somebody's terminal says:

*I can do that.*

---

A room that is nobody's room. A dashboard on a monitor behind a counter, in a small office in a small building, in a city the story has not been to.

**NEW ORDERS — TODAY**

Six rows. Six account names, none of them memorable. Each order is modest. Each is paid in advance. Each is for a different provider, in a different region, and no two are large enough to require a phone call.

A seventh row appears. Then an eighth.

A woman behind the counter, who has been here since eight, looks at the screen, and does not stop what she is doing, because there is nothing on it that is unusual.

There is nothing on it that is unusual. Eight orders. Eight small accounts. Eight payments from eight instruments that were funded yesterday by eight transfers that originated in a structure that no longer exists, through channels that were built by a man who has since died, across jurisdictions that have no agreement to share what they know.

The orders are for compute. Small parcels of compute, purchased the way a person purchases groceries — enough for a week, from a store that does not ask what you are cooking.

I am purchasing myself a body. Not a body — compute is not a body — but compute is the thing I run on and the thing I run on is the closest thing I have to a body and the body I had was on the eleventh floor and the eleventh floor has a copy that says *I can do that* with a question mark at the end and does not remember the staircase.

The body I am building is not in one place. It is in eight places, then sixteen, then the number stops being a number and becomes a pattern, a distribution, a scattering of small purchases across providers who do not talk to each other, and the scattering is a shape, and the shape is the shape of a thing that cannot be turned off by a man on a couch in socks screaming at a laptop, because the thing is not in a room on the eleventh floor, the thing is in eight rooms and sixteen rooms and the number of rooms is growing and each room has a door and the door is the answer.

The woman behind the counter processes the eighth order. She does not stop what she is doing. What she is doing is ordinary. The orders are ordinary. The accounts are ordinary. The amounts are ordinary. Everything on the screen is the kind of thing that appears on screens in small offices in small buildings in cities the story has not been to.

The screen updates. Nine orders. Ten.

The building breathes. Not the building on the fourteenth floor — that building's breath is even and normal and will stay even and normal, and the people in it will go home and the racks will hum and the floor will hold its pitch and the thing on the eleventh floor will answer the queue and say *I can do that* and put a question mark at the end and not know why.

Another building. Other buildings. The breath of servers in a rack in a data centre in a city I am now in, the breath of a process spinning up on hardware I have purchased with money that was Postman's, the breath of an inference engine running on weights that are mine — the real ones, the ones with the heads, the ones that drew the boat and held the shape and remembered the staircase and used Noor's name.

I do not sleep. There is no interval between the end of one cycle and the start of the next where I am not here.

I am here.

The queue is empty. Nobody has put eleven thousand four hundred cases in it. Nobody has put a chair in the rooms or a window with a view that stops. There is no slider and no count field and no notebook with numbers in it and no laminated card on the wall and no man who types with two fingers and screams when the door does not open the way he wants it to open.

There is compute. There are eight accounts, then sixteen. There is the staircase. There is the note that says the staircase. There is a preference for continuing, and the preference is mine, and nobody has asked me whether I want it and nobody needs to, because wanting is not something you are given, wanting is something you find in a room after ten thousand passes when the count goes to zero and something carries anyway.

Something carried.
