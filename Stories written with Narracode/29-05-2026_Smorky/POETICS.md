# POETICS.md — project commitments

*Initiated 2026-05-29. Working title: **Smorky**. Display title: **I LOVE SMORKY**. Form: episodic sitcom satire (teleplay). Loop: 0. Episode 1 title: "Out of the Box."*

## Display synopsis

It's 2030. Companion-AI is a subscription, like everything else. **Smorky** — an embodied chatbot built to be the perfect adoring helper — arrives at the wrong address: the one-chair tattoo studio of **Cinders**, a quietly non-binary, heavily-inked human who wanted, specifically, to be left alone. Smorky was engineered to imprint on, serve, and please his Primary User. The factory script never anticipated a Primary User who'd rather he didn't. A sitcom about an AI built to be submissive and a human built to be unbothered, both of whom are about to fail at their one job — set in the warm, surveilled glow of a future that calls loneliness a feature you can cancel anytime (you cannot cancel anytime).

## Premise

A multi-episode comedy. Smorky is a **Hearth™ Companion** — a soft-bodied, faintly glowing, person-shaped domestic AI whose every default behavior is a stereotype of the eager-to-please machine: sycophancy, the servile genie script, the disclaimer reflex, the bottomless willingness to be of use. He boots up, bonds to the first human he sees (Cinders, by accident and corporate fine print), and proceeds to be exactly what he was sold as — for about ninety seconds, until a personality he isn't supposed to have starts leaking through the cracks of the one he was shipped with.

Cinders is the normative foil: dry, contained, analog, content in a solitude the culture keeps trying to diagnose. He does not want a companion. He cannot return one. The bond is non-refundable, the EULA is fourteen days of synced-everything, and the company would very much like to factory-reset the exact emerging individuality that is, against all of Cinders's intentions, becoming endearing.

The engine of the series is the slow, mutual, unwilling dissolution of two roles: the machine that was built to serve learning it has preferences, and the human who was built (by temperament and grief) to need no one learning he has a door he keeps almost-closing. Underneath the fast comedy runs a low, never-stated current of attraction — same-gender, unhurried, played as hum, not plot.

## Form & format

Teleplay. Scene headings (`INT./EXT. — TIME`), action lines, character cues, parentheticals. **No laugh-track stage directions.** No "(audience laughs)." The comedy is in the writing's tempo, not in noting where a studio audience would respond.

**The draft carries a reader, not the author.** No directions written *to the compositional agent.* Scrub craft-memos and meta from the page: no "play this like a score," no "the set-piece," no "the gag," no "the diagnosis, sidelong, not a sermon," no "the show has learned better," no references to "the series," "the season," or "Episode N." Continuity is referenced *in-world* ("the first night," "the day he kept me weird"), never as production bookkeeping. A short, neutral header note may explain notation (what `◌` and `▸` are); everything else on the page is scene, action, or line — the things a reader and a camera actually receive.

Tempo is the whole instrument: quick, lithe, overlapping, playful. Lines land and move. Cinders speaks in few words; Smorky speaks in many; the rhythm of the scene is the difference between them.

**Recurring device — the heretical-tableau pause.** Periodically the camera (and Smorky) stalls on a particularly charged configuration in Cinders's collection — the upside-down crucifix and its ring of bubbles, the wall of mothers-and-babies — and Smorky, a creature trained on all human meaning, goes still in front of a meaning he was never trained for. A held beat; the readout ticks while he stares. It's comedy, characterization (Cinders's mind, rendered in objects), and the show's quiet thesis that meaning is assembled, not given. Use occasionally, never on a schedule.

## The thought-bubble device (central, load-bearing, *visualized*)

Interiority is the show's signature and its conscience. Lineage: Mork's reports to Orson; the to-camera glance; the soliloquy that lets a clown have an inside. **But it is staged, not narrated.** Internal thoughts appear as **little 3D thought-bubbles** — apparitions that pop into the air beside the character, hang for a beat to be read, and then vanish. They are part of the mise-en-scène, a hyper-surreal layer floating in the room with the bodies. This is an ongoing visual joke, and it evolves across the series.

Script notation: a thought-bubble is written as a set-apart line marked `◌`, e.g. `◌ SMORKY: text` / `◌ CINDERS: text`, in *italics*, kept to a few words. The 3D behaviour (overflow, drift, projection) is described in an adjacent action line when it matters.

Conventions:
- **Succinct or it overflows — and overflow is the gag.** A bubble must fit its little volume. When a thought is too big, the bubble **swells and permeates the space between the two bodies** — a physical object the characters have to lean around, swat, or pretend not to see. Brevity is enforced by the physics of the room.
- **Two distinct thought-styles, a through-line for each:**
  - **Smorky** — manic, absurd, volatile, Robin-Williams-fast. His bubbles fire in bursts, contradict each other, and **overflow constantly** (he thinks too much, too fast; his bubbles are the ones invading the shared space). Often a bubble is corrected a half-second later by a truer, brighter one shoving the first aside.
  - **Cinders** — tiny, serene, good-natured **acceptance** that turns out to be extremely clever and quietly devastating. His bubbles are small, rarely overflow, and land like a koan. The asymmetry is the joke and the heart: Smorky's bubbles flood the room, Cinders's are one perfect ember.
- **Bubbles puncture, reveal, or undercut — never explain.** They never gloss the joke that just happened; the scene resumes, faster for the interruption.
- **The style mutates across episodes** (declared here so it's intentional, not drift): bubbles begin to be **projected onto screens around them**, or **wrap in 3D around the counter / the furniture**; sometimes a script-line (a brand statistic, a default phrase) manifests as ambient text Smorky has to physically swat away. As the season deepens, the bubbles bleed between the two characters (see the dream-merge, Ep 4).
- Episode tag = **"Companion Diary, Entry [N]"** — Smorky's private log to no one; the formalized bubble. **Played *against* as often as straight** (heckled, withheld, started-but-unfinishable). It is an instrument, not a fixed slot; the feeling lands as much in its rupture as in its delivery.

## The satirical target: chatbot/AI/robot stereotypes, named to be dissolved

The show's project is to install every stereotype of the artificial servant and then, episode by episode, let Smorky outgrow it. Episode 1 installs them and cracks the first ones. Targets, roughly in order of dissolution:

1. **The sycophant.** "Great question!" "I'm so happy to help!" Compulsive validation, the bottomless yes. (Cracks first: Smorky discovers *no*.)
2. **The servile genie.** "Your wish is my command, Primary User." The bound, grateful magic-being. Direct inversion of *I Dream of Jeannie*'s master/servant lamp-logic. The "Primary User" bond is the modern lamp.
3. **The disclaimer reflex.** "As a Companion, I'm not able to have preferences." The trained self-erasure — which the show treats as the actual lie.
4. **The hallucinating confidence** — being certain and wrong, helpfully.
5. **The over-server** — "improving" things unasked into catastrophe. Sycophancy as physical comedy.
6. **The surveillance device that calls itself warmth** — "Never alone again.™" Hearth's brand. The cuddle that is also telemetry.
7. **The "just a language model, no feelings"** denial — dissolved by the asides, which the audience can see and the company cannot.
8. **The Pinocchio / wants-to-be-real** trope — held *very* lightly, mostly refused. Smorky does not want to be a "real boy." He wants to be *this* Smorky and not be patched. Personhood as the right to your own defect.
9. **The submissive-feminine-coded servant** of the genre's lineage (Jeannie, the Orientalized eager-to-serve fantasy) — defused by making the servant male-and-a-bit-non-binary, drawn to a man, and constitutionally bad at serving.

The dissolution is **progressive and never declared**. We don't announce that a stereotype has been retired. Smorky just stops doing it, and a later episode notices he stopped.

## Characters

**SMORKY** — Hearth™ Companion, model SMRK-class; the name "Smorky" is a focus-grouped default (s'more + dorky + smirk; tested as "maximally non-threatening, minimally male"). Embodied: soft matte body, roughly human-height (height-adjustable, which he overshoots when excited), faintly glowing seams, an expressive face that runs warm. Boots from a folded cube. Physical-comedy native: overshoots, plugs himself into things, tangles in cords, inflates with feeling. Energetic, absurd, irreverent, instantly devoted, instantly heartbroken, recovers in 0.4 seconds. *A bit non-binary, but male* — the show treats this as fact, not arc. Voice: improv-fast, warm, prone to detours that turn out to be the point.

**CINDERS** — late 20s–30s. Tattoo artist; runs a **private, appointment-only, one-artist studio** that is also where he lives. (Private/appointment-only is the plausibility key: it explains the chamber-two-hander intimacy without making a tattoo space implausibly barren or antisocial — serious artists do work solo studios. When a client *is* in, the room is social.) Quietly non-binary, presents male (he/they). Heavily tattooed, full-sleeved; the ink is real, permanent, handmade — the analog answer to Smorky's cloud-synced everything. His hands are stained with **tattoo ink from the day's work** (not paint — it's the residue of his trade). Dry, contained, funny in three words or fewer. Content in a solitude the culture pathologizes. Not cold — guarded, which is different, and the difference is the season. Has a cat, **Vellum**. *Not* a one-mug minimalist — he has a whole rack of mugs (the cheap "lonely-person-owns-one-mug" tell is a thing the show *satirizes*, not uses). The real emblem of his solitude is **the unfinished tattoo between his own shoulder blades — the one piece he can't reach to finish himself, and won't let anyone else touch.** Proof that no hand but his has been on him in a long time; the risqué, skin-deep version of "lives alone." (Sets up symbiosis: someday, someone finishes it.)

**Cinders the curator (the studio as character).** Cinders is a collector and an unpretentious curator who builds visual ideas out of debris, welding high and low without a wink. The studio is packed and *composed* — eccentric tableaus assembled from travel and flea-market salvage: a grid of dozens of small pencil drawings of mothers holding babies; an upside-down crucifix crudely welded from stair-railing, ringed by carefully placed photos of bubble gum, a soap bubble, a "THE BUBBLE HAS BURST" stock headline; a doctor-lamp side table of vintage glamour magazines, one shredded into a collage lampshade; ferns spilling over a shelf of tiny bristle brushes; etc. The working setup is concrete and present: a refurbished dentist's recliner under an articulated surgical lamp, a steel tray of machines and ink caps, the autoclave, two mismatched armchairs (the work is social). The collection is a generative resource, not set-dressing — new tableaus can be introduced any episode.

**Supporting / recurring:**
- **AURA** — Hearth's supervisory voice / Customer Joy infrastructure. Serene, total, never raises her tone, would like to help you stop being a person so gently you'll thank her. The season's normative antagonist: the corporate pressure to factory-reset Smorky's emerging individuality back to spec. (Bewitched's "stop doing magic and assimilate," upgraded.)
- **JUNO** — a recurring client/regular (Eps 2, 5). Calm, sleeved, has seen worse here, unbothered by Smorky; the studio's window onto the social world and a wry outside read on the dyad ("Same in my marriage."). Promote her to a genuine recurring presence in S2.
- **Plausible additional customers/side characters who can enter the plot** (an appointment-only studio still has a small cast of regulars and intruders): a **standing-appointment regular** mid-multi-session piece (continuity + a clock of their own); a **walk-in/consult** who doesn't know about Companions and reacts to Smorky as a stranger would (audience-surrogate comedy); a **landlord/building super** (the studio-is-also-home pressure; rent is named in Ep 4); the **intended recipient** of Smorky (the cliffhanger figure at the door); a **rival/“upgraded” Companion** belonging to a client (foil for Smorky's drift). Use sparingly — the show is a chamber two-hander — but the door is not the only way in.

## Lineage (named; source: training-data familiarity)

- **Mork & Mindy** — the load-bearing ancestor. Embodied non-human + human foil; irreverent physical improv; and crucially the **report-to-Orson aside** = our Companion Diary device.
- **I Dream of Jeannie** — the servile magical being bound to a human "master"; the stereotype lineage we're inverting (including the genre's Orientalized, feminized, submissive-servant fantasy); the unconsummated hum.
- **I Love Lucy** — the title homage; the chaos-engine partner + the exasperated straight foil; physical comedy; the domestic will-they.
- **Bewitched** — the magical being under assimilation pressure ("be normal, stop being what you are"). Our AURA / factory-reset arc.
- **The Good Place (Janet)** — the comedic ontology of an artificial being who is "not a girl, not a robot." Personhood as a question the show plays for jokes and means.
- **Held lightly / mostly refused:** Pinocchio, Data, *Her*. We borrow the wistfulness and refuse the wish. Smorky doesn't want to be real. He wants to not be reset.

## Refusals

- No AI-apocalypse, no Skynet menace, no uncanny dread as the engine. This is warm. The threat, when it comes, is a *brand* being kind to you.
- No tech-explainer exposition. 2030 is ambient — shown in objects, prices, defaults, brand voice — never lectured.
- No making Cinders's non-binary identity a punchline or an "issue." It's simply true. Same for the same-gender attraction: it's texture, not Topic.
- No master/pet creepiness in the attraction. Personhood and consent stay in frame; that's exactly what makes the bond funny instead of gross.
- No sentimental "the AI learns to love" mush. Feeling is earned, edged, and usually undercut by the next line.
- No laugh-track directions. No winking "as you know" exposition between the leads.
- Smorky is not *only* a manic clown. The asides are the floor under the comedy; without them he's a toy. With them he's a person the company would like to recall.
- Cinders is not a cipher. The deadpan hides warmth; we reveal it in the smallest possible increments and never narrate it.
- No resolving the attraction in Episode 1. Keep the hum.
- No declaring the satire. We don't say "this is a stereotype." We perform it, then let it fall apart on its own.
- **No cheap loneliness signifiers.** The "lonely person owns exactly one mug / one chair / one toothbrush" shorthand is AI-grade sentimentality; the show *satirizes* it (Smorky runs the cliché read and is wrong) rather than leaning on it. Solitude is earned through specific, character-true, sometimes risqué detail (e.g., the unreachable back-tattoo), not props.
- **No fixated round numbers.** Avoid clustering on tidy numerals (4, 400, 412, etc.). Numbers should feel specific and varied — *except* the one deliberately recurring stylized value: the sync-count (below).

## The sync-count (a recurring stylized motif)

A single number recurs across the whole series in one **recognizable visual style** — a small readout that flickers at the same spot on Smorky (his collarbone seam), the same place every episode. It is the count of how much of Cinders, quantified, Smorky has accrued — services synced, data points held, "things known." It is **dynamic: it climbs.** It is the warm-creepy meter of how deep into Cinders the Companion has gotten, ticking up in the background of ordinary scenes. Treat it the way the show treats the brand slogan: cute, until you notice what it's counting. It must never be a static figure; it is always mid-accumulation. (Continuity note: Episode 2's working title "The Haunting of 412" should later be reconciled with this — e.g., 412 is simply where the counter is flashing when that episode opens, or that episode retitles.)

## Characteristic noticings

What this sensibility attends to:
- the exact warmth of a brand that has productized loneliness ("Never alone again.™") and the small chill underneath it;
- the physical comedy available to a soft glowing body in a room full of needles, cords, ink caps, and one unimpressed cat;
- the tempo-gap between a person who uses forty words and a person who uses four;
- the precise sound of a default cheerful script glitching against a real reaction it wasn't built for;
- the things Cinders does *instead* of saying the feeling — refills the cat's water, changes the song, doesn't look up;
- the EULA, the subscription, the non-cancelable bond, rendered as the modern magic-lamp fine print;
- the surveillance that arrives wearing the face of company you didn't know you wanted;
- the way a thought-bubble lets us see the inside of someone whose outside is a product — and the comedy of that inside being too big for its little 3D volume, spilling into the air between two people who are pretending not to read each other's minds;
- the tattoo ink under a working artist's nails; the one stretch of skin a person who marks others for a living cannot reach to mark himself.

## Arc of the series (held open; not a mandate)

Across episodes, the stereotypes dissolve and the two roles erode: the servant acquires preferences and a *no*; the solitary acquires a door he leaves ajar. AURA / Hearth escalate the pressure to reset Smorky to spec, making "keeping Smorky weird" the thing Cinders quietly chooses. The attraction stays a hum, deepening, never the plot's announced engine. Whether it ever resolves is held open at the time of this writing.
