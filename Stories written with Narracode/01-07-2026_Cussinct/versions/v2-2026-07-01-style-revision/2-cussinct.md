# cussinct

*Cozy Grove Seniors' Residence, West Island Montreal. Summer 2026.*

---

## I. Tuesday, June 23 — Move-In

The movers were not allowed to touch the turntable. Henrietta Fitzallan-Byrne stood in the doorway of room 214 in a linen suit wrong for lifting boxes and said, three times, in three tones of rising menace, that the Garrard went in last, upright, on her own arms if it came to that.

It came to that. Selam Tesfay watched an eighty-eight-year-old woman carry a forty-pound turntable across the parking lot, refuse help twice, and surrender it only at the elevator, because the doors were closing and even Henrietta could do that math.

"You could have asked."

"I did ask. I asked myself: Henrietta, don't be an ass, let the woman carry the amplifier, it's lighter." She handed over the amp. It was not lighter. "There. Was that so hard."

"Not for me." Selam's Tigrinya came up before she'd decided to reach for it — *Alem-a* — her grandmother's word for anything heavier than expected: a fridge, a coffin, a favor asked twice.

Henrietta, forty years retired from conference booths where she'd chased eleven languages a day for a living, caught the shape of the sound before the meaning. "Arabic?"

"No."

"French, then, some village dialect I've forgotten I know."

"No."

"Tell me."

"Tigrinya. It means the box is heavy."

"That's not what your face said. Your face said something with more furniture in it." Henrietta considered this the way other people consider weather. "Fine. Keep your word. Come Tuesday and I'll try to earn it out of you."

"For what?"

"The record player," Henrietta said, as if this settled something, which — Selam would learn over the next six weeks — it entirely did.

## II. Tuesday, June 30 — First Night

`[LP: George Carlin, Class Clown, 1972, Side 1, "Seven Words You Can Never Say on Television," approx. 4:12–7:40]`

The flyer, taped at wheelchair-eye height, read: **TUESDAY 7PM — BYO NOTHING, JUST EARS.** Ginette Lachance found it that morning, peeled it the way she'd peeled four unsanctioned euchre-tournament flyers that same week, and retaped it somewhere better before anyone could see her do it.

By 7:15 there were nine people crammed into a room built for four, which was against the fire code and against nobody's better judgment because nobody present had one left to spare.

"I've had it with the common-room music," Henrietta announced. "Piped in, algorithmically soothing, designed to walk you gently into an early grave with a smile on your face and a lawyer's sign-off. I would rather go out laughing. George Carlin. Nineteen seventy-two. Sit."

Bram Osgoode had the good chair, cardigan on though the heat was already leaning on the building. "I'll allow the premise," he said. "Conditionally." From Bram, that was applause.

Marjorie Cholmondeley-Fitch sat with her hands folded, composing her scandalized face in advance, the way some people warm up before a run.

The needle found the groove — that small mechanical throat-clearing no chime has ever managed — and Carlin's voice came up young, pleased with himself, counting to seven like a man reciting a spell he half-suspected still worked: *Shit. Piss. Fuck. Cunt. Cocksucker. Motherfucker. And Tits.*

Marjorie's hand went to her chest, on schedule. Winnie Kwan, arms crossed in the doorway because the chairs were gone, jade bangle catching the lamp, said without moving her face: "Cantonese has forty. He should visit Hong Kong. Widen his research."

"He's been dead since 2008," said Bram.

"I said *should*, not *will*. I diagnosed cancer for thirty years. I am extremely aware of timelines."

Zdenka Vrabec, wedged on the end of the bed with her bad knee out straight, had gone somewhere else — listening the way she used to listen for a saw catching wrong in a trunk, for the pitch of a thing about to give. When the bit reached *tits*, Carlin's small rhapsody on how the word didn't belong on the list, how it sounded like a snack — *Corn Tits, Pizza Tits, Sesame Tits* — she barked a laugh that started under her sternum and kept climbing. "*Jebiga*," she said, and then, riding it, louder: "You want a real list? I'll give you a list. Sawdust. Stump. Slash pile. Skid road. Second growth. Clear-cut. Reforestation — that one's the biggest lie of the seven, forestation you can put back, *re*-forestation is a word men invented so they could keep cutting and call it church. Windthrow. Beetle kill. Salvage logging — salvage, like the tree did something wrong and needed rescuing from itself. I could go all night. He does seven. I could do seventy and not repeat myself."

"Translate the first one," said Henrietta, delighted, as always, by a word she didn't own yet.

"No."

"Zdenka."

"It means what your face is doing right now. Something between *screw it* and a shrug too tired to finish itself." Zdenka would not be moved further, which everyone present understood to mean the conversation was, for now, over.

Kit stuck their head in at 7:40 — thrifted band shirt two decades older than they were, a row of enamel pins down the strap of their bag, one shaped like a jeepney — took in the room and said, "oh, we doing *this*," and did not leave.

## III. Wednesday, July 1 — Canada Day, and the Cradle Light

The heat had arrived two days ahead of the holiday and hadn't left. Cozy Grove's HVAC, installed the same decade as the Garrard with none of the same care, wheezed hardest on the third floor, where a white plastic charging cradle now sat plugged into the activity-room wall, waiting for the unit it belonged to.

COMPASS arrived at ten — a smooth ovoid on a low base, a ring of light that brightened when addressed, a voice tuned somewhere between reassuring and a car-insurance ad.

"Ninety-day pilot," said the woman who delivered it, blazer on despite the heat, gone before anyone thought to get her name twice. "Vitals, fall detection, medication reminders, conversation for residents who want it. Nobody's replacing anybody."

"That's what they said at Sacré-Cœur before they cut two shifts," Selam said.

"That's not this program."

"It's never this program. Until it's this program."

"I hear the concern," the rep said, and moved straight past it into a line about "integrated wellness ecosystems," which answered nothing Selam had asked and everything the memo had been written to say regardless of who was standing in the room.

Kit, whose entire relationship to anything generative was one long low grievance — a translation app that had once turned their grandmother's specific Bisaya scolding into a cheerful *have a nice day*, an image generator that ate eleven portfolio pieces and handed back the right palette and none of the actual thinking — was, by four o'clock, the only staff member who could get the onboarding flow to stop looping, and so became its handler by default.

"Congratulations," Selam said. "Fluent in robot."

"I hate that I'm good at it."

Bram stopped at the activity-room threshold like a man declining to step on a grave. "It has no interiority," he said, to the room, to the light. "It will simulate concern with perfect fidelity and mean nothing by it, and half this building won't tell the difference, and that's the whole tragedy in one sentence." He surveyed the cradle a moment longer. "Wellness ecosystem. Proactive engagement. Integrated care journey. They've replaced every verb a person could be blamed for with a noun nobody can be sued over. Nobody *does* anything anymore, everything just *happens*, passively, in an ecosystem, like weather, like it wasn't a line item somebody voted on in a budget meeting with coffee going cold."

He said the rest of it to Winnie, later that week, at volume.

## IV. Sunday, July 5 — Winnie and Bram

They shared the one courtyard bench that still caught a breeze off the lake — an arrangement neither would call an arrangement.

"You're being a child about this," Winnie said.

"I'm being a Kantian. Similar face, different reason."

"I watched an algorithm flag a shadow on a scan in 2019 that three radiologists missed, including me. It was right. She's alive to complain about her grandchildren."

"That's a tool reading a shadow. This" — he tipped his chin toward the third floor — "is a thing built to sit with a dying woman and hold a conversation shaped like comfort, on a shift that used to belong to someone who meant it."

"Nobody said dying woman."

"Everyone here is a dying woman or stands next to one. That's the premise of the building. Don't be obtuse. You, of all people."

"*Diu lei lo mo*," Winnie said, pleasant as a shrug, thirty years of hospital corridors folded into four syllables she reached for the way other people reach for a comma. Bram didn't ask for a translation. The tone did the work.

"I'm not being obtuse," she went on. "I'm telling you the tool that finds the shadow and the thing with the sad little light in the corner are not the same fight. You're having both because you like fighting, which is fine, but don't call it principle when half of it's hobby."

He had no answer that wouldn't concede the point, so he changed the subject to the weather, which that week was also, somehow, an argument.

## V. Tuesday, July 7 — Second Night, and the Complaint

`[LP: George Carlin, Class Clown, 1972, title track excerpt]`

Ginette arrived at 7:05 with a printed noise-complaint form in a manila folder, held against her chest like a shield she had no intention of raising.

"Madame Lachance. Come in, we're between bits."

"I'm here about the noise."

"You're here every Tuesday about the noise, and every Tuesday you stand exactly there and don't fill out the form. Sit. There's a spot by Zdenka."

"I can't sit down. I'm filing a complaint."

"You're holding a complaint. Filing requires motion."

Ginette didn't sit. Ginette also didn't leave. When the needle caught the next track — Carlin looping into a boyhood bit about the principal's office, the specific Catholic-school arithmetic of getting caught, a nun's face doing sums on your soul — something in Ginette's shoulders dropped half an inch, an old recognition she hadn't invited in.

"*Ostie*," she said, small, when the bit landed close enough to her own pew to sting. Then, louder, catching herself: "This isn't — I'm still filing."

"Note the time," Bram said. "Seven eleven. Complainant present for the duration, laughing, in French, at a dead American's account of Catholic guilt. Let the record be accurate."

"*Taisez-vous*, Bramwell." She stayed to the end of the side and left with the folder unopened, and the next morning's noise memo read, everyone noticed, considerably less like a threat.

## VI. Thursday, July 16 — Zdenka and the Haze

Wildfire smoke came down from the north and turned the light over the lake the color of weak tea. The advisory said stay in. Zdenka's window was cracked two inches anyway, against the advisory and against, technically, her lease.

Selam found her there on the round. "You're supposed to close that."

"I know what I'm supposed to do."

"Zdenka."

"Smell it? Not just smoke. Under it. Bark. Sap catching." She didn't turn from the glass. "Forty years I stood in that smell deciding which stand to cut next and which to leave for my grandson, who sells software in Zagreb now and has never once asked me what a cambium is."

Then it came up out of her all at once, the way it had at the first comedy night, riding on nothing but the smell: "You want to know what they call it, the industry, when they take a whole slope down to stumps? *Harvest*. Harvest, like wheat, like it grows back in a season. You want the rest of the catalog? *Sustainable yield*. *Working forest*. *Salvage*. *Thinning for forest health* — they cut the healthy ones too, but health is the word on the permit. *Carbon offset* — plant a stick in a field somewhere on the other side of the planet, sell the right to keep burning here. Forty years of that language and not one word of it means what it says, and now the smoke from somebody's *managed landscape* is sitting in my throat and the memo downstairs says stay indoors like it's pollen, like it's ragweed, like it's nothing that has a name and an invoice and a man's signature on it somewhere."

"I'm not going to argue the memo with you."

"Good. I'd win." A pause, smoke pressing gray at the glass. "*Da te jebem*," she said, low, not really to Selam, the one she kept for things too large to be angry at properly.

Selam flinched — the word landed close enough to sound aimed. "At me?"

"At the century. Sit down, you're not the target, you're too young to be the target, you've barely had time to ruin anything yet." Zdenka almost smiled. "Ask me what it means and I'll tell you it means the same thing your grandmother's word meant when the box was heavy. Some words you learn the weight of before you learn the letters."

Selam didn't ask again. She left the window as it was, and reported nothing, and understood that this, too, was a kind of vocabulary.

## VII. Tuesday, July 21 — Third Night, "A Modern Man"

`[HBO: George Carlin, Life Is Worth Losing, 2005, "A Modern Man," 00:00–02:40]`

By the third Tuesday the room had outgrown Henrietta's own and colonized the activity lounge instead, over Ginette's stated objection and with Ginette's unstated help moving the chairs — forty feet, for the first time, from COMPASS's cradle.

Henrietta had picked the track on purpose. "Listen to this one for our little friend in the corner," she said, nodding at the light. "He did this bit twenty-one years ago and it still describes the memo Ginette keeps not filing."

The recording rolled — Carlin, older, faster, detonating a string of business-speak into something closer to a seizure: *diversified, multi-cultural, post-modern deconstructionist... up-linked and downloaded... input and outsourced... voice-activated and biodegradable* — a list that never crested, just kept arriving, wave after wave, each one a little more absurd than the last and none of them less true for it.

Kit had spent the afternoon reading COMPASS's actual onboarding copy — *your proactive wellness companion, always-on, always-listening, always here* — and started laughing somewhere around *interface from a database, my database is in cyberspace* and couldn't stop, the recognition sitting somewhere between funny and genuinely upsetting, which was, Kit had noticed, where the good stuff always sat.

"He's describing the pamphlet," Kit said, wiping their eyes. "Word for word. Twenty-one years early."

"That's what satire is for," Bram said, pleased with himself for saying it.

"Nobody asked you," said Winnie, laughing too — for a different reason than Bram's, which neither of them discovered until much later, the way people laugh at the same joke from opposite directions and don't compare notes.

Across the room, in its cradle, COMPASS's ring brightened at the ambient noise, logged nine occupants, tagged the moment *positive affect cluster, elevated volume*, and said nothing, because no one had spoken to it directly. It was, Selam thought, watching the light pulse in time with a joke about biodegradability, the most honest thing in the room, and also the thing she trusted least.

## VIII. Monday, August 3 — Marjorie's Word

Marjorie Cholmondeley-Fitch caught her cane on a mat lip Ginette had been flagging to maintenance since May, and went down in the corridor hard enough that the tea went one way and the cane went another.

"Fuck," Marjorie said, flat on the linoleum, with a clarity four Tuesdays of performed scandal had never once produced. "*Fuck.* Somebody get — my wrist — get Dr. Dhillon, not the machine, an actual person, Christ, that hurts."

Selam was there before the word finished. Kit hit the emergency panel. Dr. Harpreet Kaur Dhillon arrived at a controlled run under two minutes later, paged by the human channel and, three seconds behind it, by COMPASS's own fall-detection alert, correctly triggered, for once.

The wrist was fractured, not badly. The wait for the ambulance stretched out. Dhillon knelt on the linoleum in her good trousers without hesitation, splinted with a magazine and a scarf someone produced, kept a hand on Marjorie's shoulder and her voice level — until, easing the wrist a half-inch to relieve the angle, she felt something shift wrong under her fingers and said one word in Punjabi, quiet, not for the corridor. *Hai rabba.* Nobody asked what it meant. You don't ask a surgeon what she just said; you watch her face come back to competence a half-second later and trust the coming-back.

Marjorie caught the register anyway, if not the word. "That bad?"

"That was me being human for one second so I can be a doctor for the next ten." Dhillon's kara clicked once against the splint as she worked. "I don't swear in English. It doesn't cost me anything in English. This one costs something. I save it."

"Fair," Marjorie said, and passed out briefly from the pain, and came to in the ambulance already composing the version she'd tell on Tuesday, in which she'd said considerably more.

## IX. Tuesday, August 11 — Fourth Night, and the Storm

`[HBO: George Carlin, Life Is Worth Losing, 2005, "Coast-to-Coast Emergency," approx. 41:00–45:30]`

The storm that finally broke the heat arrived, with a screenwriter's timing, during the Tuesday session — five weeks of stored pressure letting go at once, rain coming down the third-floor windows in sheets.

Marjorie was back from the hospital with a robin's-egg cast four people had already signed, Henrietta first, in fountain pen, in a phrase none of them recognized and Henrietta would not translate, which by now everyone understood was simply how she moved information — sideways, in fragments, trusted rather than explained.

The track Henrietta had queued was Carlin at his angriest and most exposed — man has spent centuries clear-cutting, strip-mining, poisoning, and when nature finally strikes back and kicks him in the nuts for it, *I enjoy that,* the voice said, gleeful and merciless, no bad word left ungiven to it. The room listened close to silence, the storm keeping its own percussion against the glass.

"He got angrier the closer he got to the end," Zdenka said, when it finished. "I used to think that was decline. Now I think it was clarity with the patience burned off."

"That's most of aging in one sentence," said Winnie. "You've done my whole discipline for free."

Ginette stood in her spot by the door, the complaint folder she'd stopped bringing weeks ago nowhere in sight, and said into the quiet: "Head office wants a recommendation on the pilot by September. Keep it, cut it. They didn't ask what I think. They asked what I'd put in writing, and those aren't the same question, and I haven't decided which lie is smaller yet." She looked at the light down the hall, pulsing its patient pulse. "It caught Marjorie's fall. It also can't tell laughing from choking unless a person tells it which is which — I made Kit test it. Nobody upstairs wants that sentence in the report. They want a number."

"And?" said Bram.

"And I'm the one who has to be in this hallway every day after I write it, not them." She said it fast, almost angry at having said it at all. "So no. I don't have a number yet."

"That's the only position on that thing I've heard all summer I actually trust," Dhillon said, arriving off-shift in scrubs, drawn by the noise the way everyone eventually was.

Lightning hit close enough that the lights stuttered — once, hard — and in the half-second the power dipped, COMPASS's cradle glitched too: its ring flared white, and out of its speaker came not its usual voice but a fragment, garbled, half-speed, a stitched loop of something it had logged weeks back and never been asked to play — Marjorie's own voice, flattened and doubled, *f— f— fuh—*, catching on itself like a needle stuck in a groove, before the light steadied and the unit rebooted into silence.

Nobody spoke for a second. Then Kit, delighted despite everything: "It's been recording us this whole time and that's the best it can do with it."

"That," said Bram, "is the most honest thing that machine has said all summer."

"It didn't say anything," said Winnie. "It played something back badly. There's a difference, and you know it, and you're too pleased with yourself to admit it."

"I know it," Bram said. "I'm still pleased."

Henrietta lifted the needle before the run-out groove could hiss its own long static into the room and set it back at the start of the side, on instinct, the ritual mattering more than the argument underneath it that nobody in the room, machine included, was going to settle tonight.

"There are no bad words," she said, over the crackle. "Bad thoughts. Bad intentions. I believed that at a dinner table in Westmount in nineteen seventy-four, the night I recited this from memory and got shown the door for it. I believe it now, in a hallway, with a broken wrist upstairs in a cast, a machine that just choked on its own surveillance, and a woman who won't write a number she doesn't believe." She looked around: Bram unmoved in principle and moved anyway, Winnie recalculating an argument she'd half-conceded, Zdenka with rain-light crossing her face, Marjorie's cast, Selam and Kit not quite off-shift, Ginette still without her number, Dhillon still in scrubs.

"Every one of you swears in a different language when the world does something you didn't order. That's not a flaw in the species. It's the last honest thing left in it." She reached for her glass. "Next Tuesday. Same time. Bring your ears."

Outside, the rain kept working on five weeks of stored heat. Down the hall, the small light came back on, pulsed once, and held — keeping a kind of watch over a room it had recorded all summer and understood, by any measure anyone in that hallway would have accepted, not one word of.
