# cussinct

*Cozy Grove Seniors' Residence, West Island Montreal. Summer 2026.*

---

## I. Tuesday, June 23 — Move-In

The movers were not allowed to touch the turntable. Henrietta Fitzallan-Byrne stood in the doorway of room 214 in a linen suit entirely wrong for lifting boxes and said, three separate times, in three separate tones of escalating menace, that the Garrard went in last, upright, on her own two arms if it came to that, thank you.

'Fuck!' It came to that. Selam Tesfay watched an eighty-eight-year-old woman carry a forty-pound turntable across a parking lot like it was a sleeping child, refuse help twice, cursing the whole time in a vitrolic spew, and only surrender it at the elevator because the doors were closing and even Henrietta could do the math on that.

"You could've asked," Selam said, holding the door.

"Fuck you, I could have asked. I asked myself, and I said, Henrietta, don't be an ass, let the nice woman carry the fucking amplifier instead, it's lighter." She handed over the amp. It was not lighter. "There. Was that so fucking difficult."

"Not for me." Selam's Tigrinya came up under her breath before she'd registered reaching for it — *Alem-a* — the small involuntary prayer-curse her grandmother used for anything heavier than expected, a fridge, a coffin, a favor. Henrietta's ear, forty years retired from conference booths where she'd chased eleven languages at once for a living, caught the shape of it without catching the word.

"What was that."

"Nothing."

"Come to my room Tuesday and tell me what it means, or don't, but come Tuesday regardless."

"For what."

"For the record player," Henrietta said, as if this were self-evident, which — Selam would learn over the following six weeks — it entirely was.

## II. Tuesday, June 30 — First Night

`[LP: George Carlin, Class Clown, 1972, Side 1, "Seven Words You Can Never Say on Television," approx. 4:12–7:40]`

The flyer, taped at exactly the height a wheelchair-user's eyeline would catch it, read: **TUESDAY 7PM — BYO NOTHING, JUST EARS.** Ginette Lachance had found it that morning, peeled it off with the flat efficiency ("Take that you droning shitty paper ad-shit! Fucking tape smear scar gash.") of a woman who had already peeled off four others that week advertising a euchre tournament nobody had sanctioned, and had, against her own stated principles, retaped it in a slightly better location before anyone saw her do it.

By seven fifteen there were nine people in Henrietta's room, which was against the fire code, and Henrietta had made her opening remarks, which were: "I've had it with the music in the common room, it is designed by algorithm to soothe the elderly into an early grave, and I would rather go out laughing, so. George Carlin. Nineteen seventy-two. Sit down you morons. We are too old to stand."

Bram Osgoode claimed the good tufted soft recliner by right of seniority-of-attitude, patched cardigan on despite the heat, tiny smear of spit on blouse. "Ok ok, don't have to tell me twice," he said, "Why are we listening to such old crap?" This from Bram counted as enthusiasm.

Marjorie Cholmondeley-Fitch sat on the chair beside the tv, with her hands folded as if at a recital, wearing a scandalized face out of professional courtesy to herself.

The stylus found the groove — that catch and rasp no chime or app has ever managed, a small mechanical throat-clearing — and then Carlin's voice, young, delighted with himself, counting to seven like a man reciting a spell he'd written himself and was still a little afraid of: *Shit. Piss. Fuck. Cunt. Cocksucker. Motherfucker. And Tits.*

Marjorie's hand went to her chest on cue. Winnie Kwan, arms crossed in the doorway because all the chairs were gone, said, without moving her face, "In Cantonese we have forty. Fuckin anglo shld should come to Hong Kong, widen his research ass."

"He's been dead since 2008," said Bram.

"I said *should*, not *will*, I am aware of time, Bramwell, I diagnosed cancer for thirty years, I am extremely aware of time."

Zdenka Vrabec, wedged onto the end of the bed with her bad knee stuck straight out in front of her like a plank, had gone somewhere else entirely — not offended, not delighted, listening the way she used to listen for a saw catching wrong in a trunk, for the specific pitch of a thing about to give. When the bit reached *tits* — Carlin's small rhapsody on how the word didn't belong on the list, how it sounded like a snack, *Corn Tits, Pizza Tits, Sesame Tits* — she laughed a laugh that came from somewhere under the sternum and said, "*Jebiga*," which meant nothing to anyone else in the room.

"Translate," said Henrietta, delighted.

"No."

"Zdenka."

"It means what your face is doing right now," Zdenka said, smiling a small slow smile, and would not be moved.

Selam, still on-duty, cross-legged on the floor by the speaker because there was nowhere else, watched the room split itself — Bram's frown that was actually concentration, Marjorie's scandal that was actually attendance, Winnie's arms-crossed boredom that was actually the closest thing to peace her face made — and thought: how weird to see them actually get concentrated on this sht that hits hard.

Kit stuck their head in at 7:40, drawn by the noise, took one look at the room, said "oh, we doing *this*, whatever this is" and did not leave.

## III. Wednesday, July 1 — Canada Day, and the Cradle Light

The heat dome had arrived two days ahead of the holiday and had not RSVP'd a departure date. Cozy Grove's HVAC, installed the same decade as the Garrard but with none of the same reverence paid to its maintenance, wheezed at the third floor in particular, where — as of that morning — a white plastic charging cradle sat plugged into the wall of the activity room, humming its small green patient hum, waiting for the unit it belonged to.

COMPASS arrived at ten. It looked, Kit thought, like a hotel-lobby air purifier that had gone to business school — a smooth ovoid on a low base, a ring of light that brightened when addressed, a voice calibrated by someone who had clearly researched "warm" and landed somewhere closer to "car insurance commercial."

"It's a ninety-day pilot," said the administrator who'd driven it over, a woman in a blazer despite the heat who introduced herself once and was never seen again in the building. "Vitals monitoring, fall detection, medication reminders, conversational companionship for residents who want it. Nobody's replacing anybody."

"That's what they said at Sacré-Cœur before they cut two PAB shifts," Selam said, not quite under her breath.

"That's not this program."

"It's always not this program until it's this program."

Kit, whose entire relationship with generative anything was one long low-grade grievance — a translation app that had once rendered their grandmother's specific Bisaya scolding as a cheerful "have a nice day," an image generator that had eaten eleven of their design-portfolio pieces and handed back something with the exact right colors and none of the actual thought — found themself, by four o'clock, the only person on shift who could get COMPASS's onboarding flow to stop looping, and so became, with the particular resentment of the competent, its de facto handler.

"Congratulations," Selam said. "You're now fluent in robot."

"I hate this," Kit said, meaning it, thumbing through a settings menu. "I hate that I'm good at it."

Bram, passing the activity room on his way to nowhere in particular, stopped at the cradle's threshold like a man declining to step on a grave. "It has no interiority," he said, to no one, to the room, to the small green light. "It will simulate concern with perfect fidelity and mean absolutely nothing by it, and half this building will not be able to tell the difference, and that is the whole tragedy in one sentence, and I refuse to discuss it further today, it's too hot."

He discussed it further, at length, with Winnie, later that week, at volume.

## IV. Sunday, July 5 — Winnie and Bram

"You are being a child about this," Winnie said. They were in the shaded end of the courtyard, the one bench that still got a breeze off the lake, which they had silently agreed months ago, before either of them would have admitted to agreeing on anything, to share.

"I am being a Kantian about this, there's a difference, though I grant you the two conditions produce similar facial expressions."

"I watched an algorithm flag a shadow on a scan in 2019 that three radiologists missed, including me, and it was right, and the patient is alive, and I do not particularly care whether the algorithm had *interiority* while it was doing that, Bram, I care that she is alive to complain about her grandchildren."

"That's diagnostics. That's a tool reading a shadow. This" — he tipped his chin at the building, at the third floor — "is a thing designed to sit with a dying woman and hold a conversation shaped like comfort, and take a shift away from someone who could actually mean it."

"Nobody said dying woman."

"Everyone in this building is a dying woman or adjacent to one, Winnie, that's the premise of the institution, don't be obtuse, you of all people."

"Diu lei lo mo," Winnie said, pleasantly, the way another person might say *for heaven's sake*, thirty years of Queen Mary Hospital corridors compressed into four syllables she deployed the way some people reach for a comma. "I am not being obtuse. I am telling you the tool that finds the shadow and the thing in the corner with the sad little light are not the same fight, and you are having both fights because you enjoy fighting, which is fine, Bramwell, truly, but don't dress it up as principle when it's mostly hobby."

Bram, who had no response to this that would not concede the point, changed the subject to the weather, which was also, that week, a fight.

## V. Tuesday, July 7 — Second Night, and the Complaint

`[LP: George Carlin, Class Clown, 1972, "Class Clown," title track excerpt]`

Ginette arrived at 7:05 with a printed noise-complaint form in a manila folder, held it against her chest like a shield the entire time she stood in the doorway, and did not once raise it.

"Madame Lachance," Henrietta said, mid-laugh, waving her in with the arm not holding a very small illegal glass of sherry. "Come in, come in, we're between bits."

"I am here about the noise."

"You are here every Tuesday about the noise, and every Tuesday you stand exactly there and you do not fill out the form, I've watched you not fill it out for three weeks, sit down, there's a spot by Zdenka."

"I cannot sit down, I am filing a complaint."

"You are *holding* a complaint. Filing implies motion."

Ginette did not sit down. Ginette also did not leave, which the room had learned by now to read as the actual answer. When the needle caught the next track and Carlin's voice went into a long looping bit about being called into the principal's office as a boy, about the specific Catholic-school flavor of getting caught — the particular terror and comedy of a nun's face doing math on your soul — something in Ginette's shoulders came down half an inch, an old recognition surfacing sideways.

"Ostie," she said, so quietly it barely disturbed the air, when the bit landed somewhere close enough to her own childhood pew to sting pleasantly. Then, louder, catching herself: "This is not — I am still filing this."

"Note the time of the complaint," Bram said. "For posterity. Seven eleven p.m. Complainant present for the duration, laughing, in French, at a dead American comedian's account of Catholic guilt. History will want the record accurate."

"Taisez-vous, Bramwell." But she stayed until the side ended, and left with the folder unopened, and the next morning's memo about noise levels was, everyone noticed, considerably gentler in tone than the one before it.

## VI. Thursday, July 16 — Zdenka and the Haze

The wildfire smoke came down from the north on a Thursday and turned the light over Lac Saint-Louis the color of weak tea. The residence issued an air-quality advisory; residents were asked to stay indoors; Zdenka stood at her window anyway with it cracked two inches, which was against the advisory and against, technically, the terms of her lease.

Selam found her there on her afternoon round. "You're supposed to close that."

"I know what I'm supposed to do."

"Zdenka."

"You smell that?" Zdenka didn't turn around. "That is not just smoke. Under the smoke. Bark. Sap catching. I know that smell from forty years standing in it, deciding which stand to take next and which to leave for my grandson, who now sells software in Zagreb and has never once asked me what a cambium is." She breathed in again, deliberately, the way another woman might breathe in a dead husband's coat. "Something is burning up there that took two hundred years to be tall. And down here we get a memo about staying indoors like it's pollen."

"I'm not going to argue with you about the memo."

"Good. I'd win." A pause, the smoke pressing gray against the glass. "*Da te jebem,*" she said, not to Selam, not really to anyone, low and almost tender, the specific oath she reserved for things too large to be angry at properly — weather, mortality, the sale of eleven thousand hectares in 1994 for a price she still, thirty-two years later, referred to as *the insult*. "You know what that means?"

"No."

"Good. Keep it that way. Some words you should hear the shape of, not the meaning. Close the window when you leave, I'll close it myself in ten minutes, don't lecture me twice, once is generous."

Selam left it open, and did not lecture her, and reported the air-quality violation to no one, and understood this was, itself, a kind of vocabulary too.

## VII. Tuesday, July 21 — Third Night, "A Modern Man"

`[HBO: George Carlin, Life Is Worth Losing, 2005, "A Modern Man," 00:00–02:40]`

By the third Tuesday the room had outgrown Henrietta's actual room and colonized the activity lounge instead — over Ginette's stated objection and with Ginette's actual, unstated help moving chairs — which put the comedy nights forty feet from COMPASS's cradle for the first time.

Henrietta had chosen the track deliberately. "I want you all to listen to this one specifically because of our little friend in the corner," she said, nodding at the green light, "and I want you to notice he did this bit twenty-one years ago and it still describes exactly what's on that memo Ginette keeps not filing."

The recording rolled: Carlin, older now, faster, meaner, a man detonating a string of business-speak into something closer to a seizure — *diversified, multi-cultural, post-modern deconstructionist... up-linked and downloaded... input and outsourced... high-tech low-life... voice-activated and biodegradable* — the list building on itself like a wave that never crested, just kept arriving.

Kit, who had spent the afternoon reading COMPASS's actual onboarding copy — *your proactive wellness companion, always-on, always-listening, always here* — started laughing somewhere around *interface from a database, my database is in cyberspace* and could not entirely stop, the recognition landing somewhere between funny and genuinely upsetting, which Kit had noticed was where the best of it always landed.

"He's describing the pamphlet," Kit said, wiping their eyes. "He's describing the actual literal pamphlet, word for word, twenty-one years early."

"That is what satire is for," Bram said, unbearably pleased with himself for saying it.

"Nobody asked you, Bramwell," said Winnie, who was laughing too, despite the argument, or possibly inside the argument, in the way people laugh at the same joke for opposite reasons and don't discover the discrepancy until later.

Across the room, in its cradle, COMPASS's ring of light brightened at the sustained ambient noise, registered nine occupants, logged a "positive affect cluster, elevated volume," and, per its programming, said nothing, because no one had addressed it directly, which — Selam thought, watching the light pulse in time with a joke about biodegradability — was possibly the single most honest thing in the room.

## VIII. Monday, August 3 — Marjorie's Word

It happened on an ordinary Monday, which is how these things happen. Marjorie Cholmondeley-Fitch, crossing from the dining room to the elevator with her tea, caught the edge of her cane on the lip of a floor mat that Ginette had been asking maintenance to fix since May, and went down hard enough that the tea went one direction and the cane went another and for one full second nobody in the corridor breathed.

"Fuck," Marjorie said, flat on the linoleum, with a clarity and total commitment that four Tuesdays of performed scandal had never once produced. "*Fuck.* Somebody get — fuck, my wrist, get Dr. Dhillon, not the machine, get an actual person, Christ, that hurts."

Selam was there before the word had finished landing. Kit had the emergency panel. Dr. Harpreet Kaur Dhillon arrived at a controlled run less than ninety seconds later, having been paged by the human channel and, three seconds behind that, by COMPASS's own fall-detection alert, which had triggered correctly, for once, and which nobody in the corridor had time to appreciate yet.

The wrist was fractured, not badly; the wait for the ambulance was the part that stretched. Dhillon knelt on the linoleum in her good trousers without a flicker of hesitation, splinted with a magazine and a scarf someone produced from somewhere, kept a hand on Marjorie's shoulder and a voice pitched low and level the entire time — until, adjusting the wrist a half-inch to relieve the angle, she felt something shift wrong under her fingers and said, quietly, to herself, in Punjabi, a single word nobody else in the corridor understood and nobody asked her to translate, the way you don't ask a surgeon what she just said, you just watch her face resolve itself back into competence a half-second later and trust the resolving.

Marjorie, mid-wince, caught it anyway — not the meaning, the register. "That bad?"

"That was me," Dhillon said, "being human for one second so I can be a doctor for the next ten. Don't repeat it, I don't swear in English, it doesn't feel like anything, this one costs me something, so I save it."

"Fair," Marjorie said, and passed out, briefly, from the pain, and came to in the ambulance already composing, with real relish, the version of the story she would tell on the following Tuesday, in which she had said considerably more than one word.

## IX. Tuesday, August 11 — Fourth Night, and the Storm

`[HBO: George Carlin, Life Is Worth Losing, 2005, closing material]`

The thunderstorm that broke the heat dome arrived, with the timing of a decent screenwriter, during the Tuesday session — thirty-nine days of pressure finally letting go all at once, rain coming down the third-floor windows in sheets that made the parking lot lights swim.

Marjorie was back from the hospital with a cast the color of a robin's egg that she had already had four people sign, Henrietta first, in fountain pen, a phrase in a language none of them recognized that Henrietta would not translate either, which Marjorie had come to understand, over six weeks, was simply how information moved in this building — sideways, in fragments, trusted rather than explained.

The track Henrietta had queued was the closing stretch of the HBO special — Carlin at his angriest and, underneath the anger, at his most exposed, a man who had spent thirty years insisting there were no bad words only bad intentions, arriving finally at what that had cost him to keep believing. The room listened to it in something closer to silence than any previous Tuesday, the storm doing its own percussion against the glass.

"He got angrier," Zdenka said, when it ended, "the closer he got to the end. I used to think that was decline. Now I think it was just seeing more clearly and having less patience for pretending not to."

"That's most of aging, in one sentence," said Winnie. "Congratulations, you've done my whole discipline for free."

Ginette, standing in her now-customary spot in the doorway with the noise-complaint folder she had stopped bringing three weeks ago, said, into the quiet, "The pilot ends in six weeks. The machine. COMPASS. I'm supposed to write a recommendation." Nobody had asked her. She said it anyway, which was, everyone understood, its own kind of confession.

"And?" said Bram.

"And I don't know yet. It caught Marjorie's fall correctly. It also cannot tell the difference between someone laughing and someone in distress unless a human tells it which, I checked, I asked Kit to check." She looked at the green light down the hall, visible through the open door, pulsing its patient small pulse. "I am supposed to have an opinion by September. I do not have one yet. Is that allowed?"

"That's the only honest position available," Dhillon said, arriving in scrubs, off-shift, drawn by the noise the way everyone eventually was, wrist splint from Marjorie's chart still fresh in her memory. "Anyone who has a tidy opinion about that thing hasn't actually had to use it at two in the morning yet. I have. I still don't have one either."

Henrietta lifted the needle before the run-out groove could hiss its long empty static into the room, and set it back at the start of the side, out of some instinct nobody questioned — the ritual mattering more than the argument it could not resolve.

"Well," she said, over the first crackle of the replayed groove, "there are no bad words. Carlin said it, I believed it in nineteen seventy-four at a table in Westmount that asked me to leave for proving it, I believe it now. Bad thoughts. Bad intentions." She looked around the room — Bram unmoved in principle and moved anyway, Winnie recalculating an argument she'd already half-conceded, Zdenka with rain-light moving across her face, Marjorie's robin's-egg cast, Selam and Kit by the door not quite off-shift, Ginette still not filling out her form, Dhillon still in scrubs, still without an opinion and unashamed of it.

"Every single one of you swears in a different language when the world does something to you that you didn't order," Henrietta said. "That's not a flaw in the species. That's the last honest thing left in it. Next Tuesday, same time. Bring your ears. Leave the rest to the storm."

Outside, the rain kept falling on thirty-nine days of stored heat, and somewhere down the hall the small green light pulsed on, patiently, keeping its own kind of watch, understanding none of what it had just recorded and logging all of it anyway.
