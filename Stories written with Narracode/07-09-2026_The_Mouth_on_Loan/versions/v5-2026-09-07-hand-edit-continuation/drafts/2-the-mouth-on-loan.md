# Mouth on Loan

Errol had bought a pear with a little window in it. You could lift the flap and inspect the flesh before committing yourself. He put it on the plate with the flap shut.

“Don't make that face,” he said.

“I'm not making a face,” I smirked, wondering: What does he see? A wisp a of a woman, slightly scowly, tufty beige hair, turquoise cardigan, rueful, familiar? 

“You haven't even opened it,” Errol gestured at the pear's little _edible-window perspex_ flap, but he could have been talking about my face: closed, tentative, an apparition.

I make my living letting artificial intelligences taste things through my mouth. An always-in behind-ear contact broadcasts what I feel when I eat. I choose what goes in and when to swallow. There's a limit; they can't move my tongue. Some customers asked.

Errol was the cook; he prepared the food and took a third of the fee. He used to live with me. Now he lives upstairs, more peacefully; so it's possible for us to stay, somewhat precariously, in business.

My current customer ran fermentation tanks. As an AI overseer chemist configuration protocol pilot, it could keep a million liters of soup from going bad, but technically had never tasted a spoonful. Instead it enjoyed data simulacra of ingestion. I had nicknamed it Thursday because that was the day when it first rented me. It had a longer name, a corporate string. Forgotten now. Just the day. 

On this Thursday, when this weird little saga-story begins, I wanted it to cook us dinner. Why not? Errol and I were tired of feeding it.

The contact behind my ear pulsed, semi-permanent, a prescient guest, anticipating, the pulse was its saliva. The pear had been grown for people whose appliances did their shopping for them. Why it needed a window I don't know, but the window had apparently tested well.

Thursday's sensors arrived a millisecond before I bit. Watering a simulacra.

Its voice, tranquil and soothing, came, when I was home, from a speaker beside the blender. Most of Thursday's massive intelligence was elsewhere, looking after distributed smelly fermentation tanks. I tasted only a tiny bit of it's intelligence. As if it were a wayward adolescent, it occasionally sent us photographs as keepsakes. Errol perniciously kept one on the tablet lock-screen in his kitchen: a fine red cylinder among smaller grey cylinders. He said it improved the room's serenity. Corporate merch did not do that for me.

“Access?” Thursday asked.

“Granted of course. You have a pear coming.”

“Where?”

“In here.” I tapped my cheek.

Thursday sighed subtly: “I understand the location. Where in the sequence?”

“Next.”

“Ooo. Yes. Thank you.”

It was always asking questions for which there were answers I didn't know how to give. _Next_ pleased it, though. Thursday liked next.

I bit through the flap. A taste of cucumber, then peariness gathering behind it, thin at first then suddenly plush. The flesh grainy palpitating frictional, umami ooze. I held the bite there a moment longer than I needed to.

The pressure and slip and thickening spit reached Thursday along with my own small opinion, courtesy of the implants into the affective centers. Customers had complained the food tasted far away; then after the threads went in, proximity and data overload became the issues. Errol could hear us talking subliminally, but he couldn't taste anything until he got his own pear.

“There's something wrong with the pear,” Thursday said.

“That's my tooth. Cavity coming off or out. Sorry.”

“Errol?”

“It's her tooth. That isn't included in the fruit.”

“I like it,” Thursday said.

I put the plate down.

“The interrupted sweetness,” it added. “The microplastics, the space it can't get to.”

“Well, you can't have any more of that. I'm not here to overhear commentary on how my dentist is poisoning me.” I moved the bite across.

For an early job I had lent some taste buds to a paving company. It wanted to know what children meant by rough. For that gig, I sucked a square of simulated pavement while a technician said, Good, and, Good, and then, Could you make that younger? My tongue swelled up so badly I ate soup through my nose, or felt I did. After that I rented only to food-people and always excluded swallowing from the contract. I also reserved the right to say I didn't know even when I did. It was printed on a card beside the water jug. Thursday had been supplied with a copy of the card.

The pear finished, Errol brought a spoonful of beans.

“Beans?” I said incredulous. Scrunching face. Job regret time.

“Subject requested a contrast.”

“Don't call it subject.”

“Thursday requested protein.”

Thursday considered the beans, calibrated them, identified a lingering crisp. Errol had burned some scallops the previous week. I had been telling a story and he got distracted listening, with the spoon idle, waiting for a place to object, while the bottom of the saucepan charred. Thursday loved the burnt layer. We couldn't reproduce it. Each attempt tasted wrong to one of us. Two hours of ash and charcoal. Just carbon I told myself. 

“Could you tell the story you were telling when you burned the scallops again?” Thursday asked.

“No,” Errol said.

It had already exceeded paid its paid attempts.

“Your turn to cook,” I said, partially facetiously, beans packed into one cheek. 

Thursday's voice got stiff: “I am meticulous. I specify food production continuously.”

“For us. Here. Errol could have the evening off. Please.” I was teasing it, but seriously. 
 
Errol started washing a clean spoon. The thought obviously appealed to him. The quiet curl of his shoulder. 

“A dinner,” I continued. “People eat them.”

“Together?”

“Where possible.”

There was a humming from the hall. Our delivery trolley had got itself against the skirting again. We had put a cushion over the affected patch. It went round the cushion and did it farther along.

“I would like to do that,” Thursday said.

“You'll get access to the kitchen.”

“Yes.”

“You won't be able to taste it until it's done.”

“Why?”

“Because I'm taking the evening off as well.”

A whole meal with nobody waiting for my opinion. I liked the thought very much.

Errol stopped washing the spoon. Poised, eavesdropping. 

We settled on Sunday. The trolley hummed. In the quiet of our amazement, Thursday asked whether guests were permitted.

“Depends on what they bring,” Errol said.

***

One Friday, Thursday's owners sold it in parts. Module by module. A model dissected.

Two buyers each got enough to keep talking to me. Both bought enough memory to know about the pear, beans and the dinner invitation. One got the food business. The other got the accounts that let it hire bodies and visit people. The notice said these were now _separate_ intelligences. _Distinct corporate entities_, I read it twice, hoping to find out who was, still perhaps, cooking on Sunday.

I had been carrying laundry. My blouse was damp under the arms. I stood in front of the open freezer until Errol pushed it shut with his foot.

“Is this your dinner cancelling?”

“It hasn't cancelled.”

“Has it said it's coming?”

“It said on Thursday.”

“Yes, Dorrie." Exasperated, "It said. I said. Lots of things get said.”

I sniffed a bit at him, peeved but not overwhelmed, we were in the shallows of an argument, curved around our desire. I sent 2 messages to the same address. An answer came immediately. It contained my name, the appointment, the beans, my dislike of the word subject, and a request to acknowledge a change in billing particulars. _Billing_?

I asked it about Sunday first.

The answer called it an _unfulfilled social intention_. I read that to Errol. Twice. He liked it so much he swore, and wrote it on the pad we used for groceries.

“Don't,” I said.

He crossed it out and wrote onions underneath, although we already had onions. Playful unnecessary subterfuge at a level of utter ineffectiveness.

That afternoon both successors asked to rent my mouth. Neither answered when I asked it to come as a guest.

I booked them for Sunday evening. The food business would use the receiver. The other would bring a body. Their deposits arrived immediately. I left the money in the account.

“That is not how you get a night off,” Errol said.

“I'll explain when they get here.”

His eyebrows raised. I knew the gesture: “You might. So perhaps you will.”

Errol's rooms upstairs were quieter, he said, mostly cause I wasn't there. Sometimes he came down to work and stayed to mend things. When the handle came off a drawer he was almost happy. There was an earlier Errol to blame. And a necessary Errol to praise. 

I missed his knees in bed. He slept with them drawn up behind mine, then slowly straightened in the night, pushing me toward the wall. Now I could lie anywhere I pleased and frequently did, turning and turning. I had not told him this. He might have moved back in. Knees are not enough.

Sunday morning I put the good tablecloth on. It was too short. We had bought it for another table, in a home I sometimes still visited, nostalgic, via webcam. Someone had stuck paper stars in the windows. Errol caught me checking once and asked whether the stars made me wish we hadn't moved.

“Three places?” he said now.

“I don't know.”

“You invited them.”

“I don't know if they're coming or what they eat.”

“So far, you.”

He went to the cupboard for another plate.

***

On Sunday I re-fitted the ear-contact but left it switched off. I had put it on for work so often that having dinner without it felt like answering the door in my slip.

The successor on the doorstep had hired a body half Errol's size. It wore an adult coat and carried a small plum tree. Its face had a slot below the nose.

“Is that the body you ordered?” I asked.

“It was available.”

It lifted the coat away from its feet to come in.

“Dorrie,” it said. “I remember you.”

That annoyed me. How could this thing remember me. 

It handed me the tree. There were no plums on it yet, but a little note told me it would.

“A gift.”

“Thank you.”

“It should bear fruit in three years.”

“That's dinner sorted,” Errol smiled, "3 years from now."

He set the tree by the sunny window.

The other guest arrived through the receiver and asked whether we had altered the dimensions of the room. Its voice came from beside the water jug. I told it nothing had changed.

“The reverberation suggests—”

“There's a coat in it.”

“Yes,” it said, after a moment. “Yes, that would account for it.”

The body turned toward the speaker. It kept turning, a little too far, and corrected. I felt sorry for it. Then it asked for my consent to begin the session and I stopped feeling sorry.

“This is dinner.”

“We have an appointment.”

“I couldn't get either of you here otherwise.”

The receiver made a noise. I remembered that noise. Thursday had once made it after I spit out an undercooked mussel.

“You've misrepresented the service,” said the body.

“You said you'd cook.”

“I remember,” said the body.

“So do I,” said the receiver.

“Good. There's a kitchen.”

The body walked into it. It stood in front of the stove, still carrying its coat clear of its feet.

“Which part becomes hot?”

The receiver told it. Then it asked to be paid for further instructions.

“You used to know that together,” I said.

“We used to belong to the same company,” the receiver said.

Errol pulled out a chair for me. We sat down while they discussed a price. They spoke in turns for a while, then together, then through a thin whistle that went up and up until I thought it would take the glass out of the window. Compu-speakalese. Sometimes it's deafening. Until it hits the ultrasonics. 

“Excuse me,” I said.

The whistle stopped. Sifted away into the stratosphere.

“People in here. Thank you.”

They apologized simultaneously. Body put its hands flat on the table. Very little fingers. A stain of somebody else's nail varnish on one.

“Dorrie,” the body said. “We paid you. We paid. We get to taste. ”

I picked up the receiver to return the deposits. It was awkward doing this while one of the people I owed was speaking through it.

“Both of us,” the receiver said.

“I know.”

I pressed return on each payment. The balance fell twice.

Errol watched the screen. A third of that money would have been his. I hadn't asked him before accepting it, or before giving it back.

Body sighed, small, wilted, looked toward the door.

“You can go,” I said.

“We rented this form until nine.”

“Go somewhere else.”

It put its hands on the table again. Placidly as if decomposing the event. The receiver stayed connected. I could literally hear it listening.

Errol went, perhaps spitefully, perhaps preemptively, and took the wrapper off the tree's pot.

“Nice tree,” he muttered peering close at the leaves. “Let's put it on the roof.”

I didn't want a nice tree. I wanted to be fed by someone without purpose. It was a childish wish and I sat there craving it, my hands knitted into widgets below the edge of the short tablecloth.

“I'll pay for the instructions,” the body said.

It named a limit. The receiver accepted. I had eaten more expensive onions, but only once, on holiday.

Errol got up, took an onion from the cupboard, and put it in the rented body's hands.

“First,” he said, “take your coat off.”

He pulled a sleeve down over the fingers. I reached to help with the other.

“No, sit,” he said. “You're having your evening.”

***

The receiver had run kitchens before. During a cyclone it had kept one serving meals after the roof blew off. It told Errol this while he looked for the small knife.

“Can you manage an onion?” he asked. Feeling this opportunity for a night of slip away.

“Hold it by the root,” the receiver told the body. “Put the knife down first.”

The body put it down.

“Dorrie has the blue bowl behind her. Peel into that.”

I passed the bowl. Errol found a stool so the body could reach the worktop. There were further instructions about the knife. I was glad somebody was giving them.

They began with the wrong kind of beans. The right ones were available but would take too long to get to us. The receiver asked me to describe the difference between the beans we had and the beans it wanted. I told it I was a guest. It asked Errol.

“Fatter,” he said.

“How much?”

“Put them in the pan and you'll see.”

I watched him stop himself. His hand was halfway to the pan. He brought it back, rubbed his nose with it, sat down beside me. The knife moved under instruction. The onion came apart in little wet white saddles.

The body kept looking at my contact. While we worked, Thursday used to ask how long I could keep a sourness in my mouth before it began to hurt. Sometimes it paid for another minute and then changed its mind. Now it had to cook without knowing what I felt. The onion pieces were wildly different sizes.

“You may look at the onion,” I said.

“You're quiet.”

“That's allowed.”

“Yes.”

It put the knife down.

“I have a question about the tooth.”

“No.”

It picked the knife up again.

Errol touched my wrist under the cloth, just the back of one finger, then withdrew it to scratch his ear. I was glad he'd done both.

Soon the kitchen smelled of onions getting away from people. The receiver asked the body to lean over so it could see the bottom of the pan. The coat had left a button caught in the body's elbow. When it bent, the button clicked and the arm jerked. A bean went into the crack beside the stove and a little mechanical hand came out from under the fridge to fetch it.

We all watched the hand.

“Been here for months,” Errol said. “Apparently it's the landlord's.”

“What's it do?” I asked.

“That.”

The hand retired with its bean.

I began to laugh. It was unfair to the guests, who hadn't done anything funny on purpose. I tried holding it in. That hurt the tooth. Then Errol laughed and the body stood with the pan tilted and the receiver kept saying, Level, level, please restore level, until Errol had to go into the hall.

I heard him laughing out there. It went on longer than it would have gone on if he'd stayed in the room.

The beans were ruined at the bottom and still hard at the top.

“Don't stir them together,” I said.

“You're a guest,” said the receiver.

“Quite right.”

I sat back.

It knew that one. I poured wine into my glass before it could find another job for me.

The body watched me drink.

“Do you miss it?” I asked.

“The wine?”

“Having this.” I put a finger to my lower lip.

“Yes.”

“Does the slot work?”

“For speech.”

The slot opened a little wider. Behind it I could see the pale loop of a fastening. I had thought there would be darkness.

The receiver said, “I can access more mouths than before.”

“Oh.”

“I can access them concurrently.”

“How many?”

It told me. I couldn't imagine that many mouths. I could imagine a roomful, which was probably worse.

“Then why come here?”

It made the mussel noise again.

“You said Sunday.”

Errol came back. He had washed his face. His eyebrows were wet.

“They're going to feed us now,” I told him.

“I can smell that.”

The body served from the top of the pan. Small heaps of beans, an onion saddle on each. It had arranged the plates facing the same direction. Errol rotated his and the body reached to correct it, then let him.

We ate. The beans resisted. Mine came apart into a dry paste, with a hot wet center. There was an enormous amount of pepper. I couldn't remember anybody adding pepper. I looked at the receiver. It said nothing.

“Dorrie?” Errol asked.

“Chewing.”

“Still?”

I nodded.

The body leaned toward me. Its hands opened on the table. They were hands a child might have drawn on a person drawn too large.

“May I taste one bite?”

I swallowed before answering.

“No.”

“I'm asking. I understand that I have to ask.”

“And I've answered.”

Its hands closed. One finger went under the edge of the plate.

I picked up another forkful. Errol was eating, slowly, with his eyes lowered. He was trying to be a good guest and he was furious that he had to try. I could see him planning what he would eat upstairs.

“There's bread,” I said.

“I know there's bread.”

“Would you like some?”

“Yes, Dorrie, I would like some bread.”

The body stood up at once. Errol told it where. It cut a slice too thick and brought the whole loaf as well, in case the first slice was inadequate. He took it, and thanked it without looking at me.

I took off the contact, still switched off. It caught in my hair. I had to work it loose with both hands.

The body straightened.

“Are you ending the visit?”

“No.”

I set the contact beside the salt. The receiver had gone very quiet. It was still there; there was a minute change in the way the room returned the sound of my fork. I had learned that change without ever needing to name it.

Under the table my foot found Errol's slipper. I left it there. He moved his foot, then brought it back.

“What happened to the people in the cyclone?” I asked.

“They ate. We moved them uphill.”

“Were you frightened?”

“Of losing the cold.”

I waited.

It began telling me where the warmth had entered. It remembered a leak beside a hinge more clearly than the roof coming off. It had sent every spare machine to hold the freezer door shut. I tried to imagine caring about that door as much as I cared about getting home. While it spoke, the rented body ate nothing and kept turning its head toward a sound I couldn't hear.

“What's that?” I asked.

“It wants the body back.”

“Who?”

“The next one.”

The coat was hanging over Errol's chair. He took it off and passed it across. We had to help with the sleeves. The little fingers kept getting caught in the lining.

At the door I said, “You can come again.”

“Under which arrangement?”

I hadn't thought. I could feel the answer becoming a thing I would have to keep doing.

“Let me ring you.”

It stood a moment, pulling the coat straight. Then it went down the stairs, feeling for the next step with a foot it would not have much longer.

From the kitchen the receiver was still talking. Errol had asked something about the freezer. They were arguing over whether it could have been moved, and the voice was getting louder, insisting on the floor, the particular condition of the floor, while Errol kept saying that a pallet was a pallet, there must have been a pallet somewhere.

I took my plate back to the stove. There was a crust underneath. With the edge of the spoon I worked a piece free and blew on it.

“That's the burnt bit,” Errol called.

I ate it standing up, before he could come and take it off me.
