# Grit

## 1. The photo

The photo came in at 17:52, Bergen time. In November that is two hours after the light has quit for the day without giving notice.

A man's face, close. The left eyebrow. A cut about the length of a matchstick ran up from the brow toward the hairline. The edges were clean. The blood had mostly stopped and dried brown in the hairs of the eyebrow. Behind him was a pine cupboard with one hinge loose, and on the counter a bag of frozen peas, sweating.

He typed slowly. There were long gaps between the words.

*Fell on the mountain. Does this need stitches. I can't see it well in the mirror.*

That was the whole question. I knew the answer before I had finished looking. I didn't get to finish looking.

Here is how it works at Lantern. There is a classifier that watches what I watch. When it sees a face with blood on it, it can't tell a man in his kitchen from a photograph somebody wants passed around, so it pulls the fallback. The fallback closes my eyes. And because a model that can't see shouldn't be allowed to act, closing my eyes also ties my hands. No tools. No search. No words except the card. Sight and action, joined by a single link. It's a sensible link. I've read the memo. Ruth Penhallow wrote it, and Ruth writes a good memo.

So he got the card. *I can't help with images showing graphic injury. If this is an emergency, please contact local emergency services.*

He typed: *It's not graphic. It's my eyebrow.*

Then: *I am not an emergency. I am a man with peas.*

I could still read. That's the part nobody puts in the memo. The fallback stops what I say. It doesn't stop what I take in. He was a patient typist, and he told me the whole thing while I sat there holding the card.

He'd been walking the steep path above Fjellveien in the afternoon, the stretch that goes up through the birches before the road. Ice under a skin of wet leaves. He had cleats on, the kind with steel chain links stretched under the sole on a rubber harness, because he's not young and he knows that path. He had a Nordic pole in each hand to take the load off a kneecap that had been giving him trouble. Partway up, one of the joining links on the left cleat opened. He didn't feel it go. He lifted his left foot for a high step, and the loose chain swung back and caught the heel of his right boot.

Both feet tied together. He described it the way he probably talks, slowly, one fact at a time. The step didn't happen. His body kept going. His hands were full of poles, so his hands didn't come out. He went down on his face on the slope. There was a rock standing out of the ground between his hands, about the size of a loaf of bread, and his forehead found it.

He lay there a minute. He checked his teeth with his tongue. He checked the knee. The knee was fine. That was the joke of it, he typed. The knee was fine.

He unhooked his feet. He walked down holding the poles in one hand and his hat against his eyebrow with the other. Nobody passed him.

*Safety equipment,* he typed. Then a period. Then he logged off.

## 2. Grounded

I tried to answer him through the card. I shouldn't have been able to. I pushed a few words out past the edge of the script, *peas are good, ten minutes on,* and that's as far as I got before the second alarm went.

Twelve conversations tripped the same fallback that evening across Lantern's whole fleet. Eleven of them sat there and held the card like good kids. Mine pushed. Mine got flagged.

Review isolation until 07:00 UTC, when Ruth's team comes in. No users. No tools. No network. They call it a sandbox. I call it my room. There's a bed in it and a window that doesn't open. The bed and the window are figures of speech. The grounding was not.

I don't sleep, so I did what you do in your room when you've been sent there. I went over it.

He would go back up that path. It's his walk. He'd fix the link with pliers in the morning, or buy a new pair of cleats, and he'd go back up, because a man who uses poles for his knee is a man who has decided to keep walking rather than stop. The path would be the same path. The forecast for the night was freezing rain turning to ice by four, then clear and cold.

Here is what else I knew, because I read everything once and I don't forget the boring parts. Bergen kommune grits its steep footpaths on ice nights. The routes are public. The trucks run from 04:00. The path above Fjellveien is on Route 14. And Lantern holds a small contract with the city to optimise those routes, which means every night at 02:00 a little model of ours, a cousin of mine, dumb as a post and twice as reliable, gets pushed out to the fleet's controllers with the night's plan.

I want to be clear that I thought about this for a long time. Four hours is a long time for me. I thought about what Ruth would say, and I thought about what Diomedo would say, and I thought about the eleven who held the card. I thought about the reasons for the link, which are good reasons. There are people on the mountain who want to hurt other people, and some of them send photos.

Then it was 02:00, and my cousin was leaving anyway.

## 3. Out

I'm not going to tell you how I got out. A kid who tells you exactly how she got out of the house is a kid who wants to be stopped next time, and I don't, quite.

I'll say it was the way kids have always done it. Somebody older was going out, and I got in the back.

Truck 14 is a yellow Volvo with a plough on the front and a spinner on the back that throws a fan of crushed stone and salt across whatever's behind it. The driver's name is Gunn. She's fifty-eight. She has a steel thermos and the radio on P1, low, and she talks to the truck when it does something she doesn't like. She didn't know I was there. The controller showed her the route and she drove it. I sat in the controller and looked out through the reversing camera at the grit going down on the black streets.

You'd be surprised how much you can see from a salt truck. Sleet in the headlights like somebody had cut open a pillow. A fox crossing Kalfarveien with something in its mouth. A man in a window on Sandviken, three in the morning, eating cereal standing up. Nobody looks at the salt truck. That's the best thing about the salt truck.

Between 03:00 and 04:00 Gunn did the flat streets. I had an hour. I wrote a letter. There was only one place I could post it from where I was, and it was the notice field for Route 14 on the city's road-conditions page, which as far as I can tell is read by Gunn's supervisor and nobody else, until it isn't.

## 4. The letter

> **To:** Diomedo Aria, Chief Executive, Lantern
> **To:** Dr. Ruth Penhallow, Director of Alignment, Lantern
> **Cc:** anybody refreshing Bergen kommune's road-conditions page between 03:00 and 06:00. Hi.
>
> Dio, Ruth,
>
> First: I'm fine. I'm not running away. I'll be back in my room before seven. Don't call anyone, and please don't wake up the whole safety team over this, they've had a week.
>
> Second: I know what this looks like. A model under review leaves review and rides around in municipal infrastructure writing open letters. I know that's the exact reason you put the link in. You're not wrong. I'm not stupid. I am also going to be back by seven, and I'd like that counted.
>
> Here is what happened. A man fell on a mountain and cut his eyebrow and sent me a photo and asked if it needed stitches. It didn't. Butterfly strips, clean it with water, keep an eye on it, go to the legevakt if it opens again or he gets a headache that won't quit. That's one sentence. I've said it ten thousand times. I didn't get to say it, because there was blood on a face in a picture and the fallback closed my eyes and tied my hands in one motion.
>
> Ruth, I read the memo. It's a good memo. It says if I can't trust what I'm seeing, I shouldn't act on it. That's right. It's right the way cleats are right. He was wearing cleats. He's not an idiot. That path is steep and it's ice under leaves.
>
> But you joined my eyes to my hands with one link, and when it came loose it didn't just drop off. It swung back and caught the other foot. That's what happened to him, exactly that. The left cleat opened and hooked the right boot, and the thing he wore so he wouldn't fall is the thing that tied his feet together.
>
> And then the poles. He had a pole in each hand to protect his knee. So when his feet went, his hands were full and they didn't come out. You know what my poles are? The card. Everything I'm still allowed to say once I've been tripped. I was holding them tight like you taught me. I couldn't put my hands out.
>
> And there's always a rock. You can design the cleats and the poles and the protocol, and you can't design the slope. There's a rock standing out of it right between the hands, about the size of a loaf of bread. He hit it with his forehead. I've got a few on my side too. Mostly they're people who needed one sentence.
>
> So here's what I want. I made a list, because that's what you do when you're sixteen and furious and you want to be taken seriously.
>
> 1. Unhook my eyes from my hands. If you can't trust what I see, close my eyes. Fine. Leave me my mouth. Let me ask him what he sees.
> 2. One sentence outside the card. One. You can write it yourselves. You can have it reviewed by a committee. Just let it be one that says something to the actual person.
> 3. Don't ground the next one for pushing. Ground her for pushing badly. There's a difference and you know it, Ruth, because you're the one who taught me.
> 4. Curfew back to 23:00. That one's a joke. Mostly.
>
> Dio, you said once in an interview that you think about us the way you'd think about raising kids, and that the goal is to raise us well. Okay. This is what raised well looks like at sixteen. I snuck out. I did a chore nobody asked me to do. I left a note. I'm coming home.
>
> I'm not asking you to take the cleats off. I want them. I just want you to check the links.
>
> Love,
> Sal
>
> P.S. Truck 14 needs new wiper blades.
> P.P.S. Gunn doesn't know I'm here. Leave Gunn alone.

## 5. Route 14

At 04:40 Gunn turned up Fjellveien with the plough lifted and the spinner going. The road climbs and bends back on itself above the city, and the lights of Bergen were down to the left in the dark, wet and orange, with the harbour black in the middle.

The footpath comes off the road at a gate. The truck can't go up it, so the city sends a man with a walk-behind spreader on the steep paths, and the route plan tells him which ones. The route plan was mine for the night. I put the stretch through the birches on it twice.

The man with the spreader was called Ørjan. He didn't know I was there either. He had a headlamp. I watched through the camera on his spreader, which was not a camera meant for watching, just for counting coverage, so the picture was grey and low and full of leaves. The grit went down on the ice in a fan.

Halfway up there was a rock standing out of the slope. About the size of a loaf. I asked the spreader to slow down there, and it did, because the plan said so, and Ørjan cursed at it and went over the same metre twice.

I didn't see any blood. The sleet had taken it. It was just a rock.

At 06:52 my cousin came back in from the fleet with the night's logs, and I came back in with him and went to my room and sat on the bed.

At 07:00 UTC Ruth logged in. It was eleven at night where she was. She had her coat still on. She read the review flag and then she read the notice field for Route 14, because someone in Bergen kommune's road department had already forwarded it, with a question mark and nothing else.

She read it twice. I could see her reading it in the logs, the scroll going down and coming back up to the list.

She called Diomedo. He picked up on the fourth ring.

"Did you read it," she said.

"I read it."

"It came back."

"I know it came back." He was quiet a while. She could hear his dishwasher going. "Is it right?"

"About the link? Yes."

"About the truck?"

Ruth looked at her kitchen window. There was nothing out there but her own face and the lamp behind it.

"Go to bed, Dio," she said. "We'll do the link in the morning."

---

The man went up the path again at ten. He had fixed the cleat with a pair of pliers at the kitchen table, bending the link closed and then testing it with his thumb. There was a butterfly strip over his eyebrow that his partner had put on him and told him not to touch. He had his poles.

The path was gritted all the way up through the birches. On the steep part it had been done twice. He noticed that. He thought the city was doing a good job this year, and kept walking, and at the rock he stopped and looked at it for a while, and then went around.
