# The Green Interregnum

> "Responsibility to the tree makes everyone pause before beginning."
> — Robin Wall Kimmerer, *Braiding Sweetgrass*, p. 152

The city has decided to let everything grow.

This was announced two summers ago as policy — *Urban Canopy Emergency Directive 14* — but policy is a thin word for what has actually happened, which is closer to a surrender negotiated in good faith. Bindweed has taken the parking structures. Ailanthus, the ghetto palm, the tree of heaven, rises out of every seam in the asphalt like the asphalt was only ever a lid. The municipal position is that shade is infrastructure now, that every degree of canopy is a body not carried out of a bus shelter at four in the afternoon, and so the mowers have been sold and the herbicide contracts allowed to lapse, and the city has gone soft and furred at every edge, a machine growing its own pelt.

I walk in the early mornings, before the wet-bulb advisories go amber. This is the hour the city belongs to the walkers, the shift workers, the machines, and the unhoused, which is to say the hour it belongs to everyone the daytime economy has no immediate use for.

The headlines arrive at the lower left of the visual field, small and tasteful, like moths that have learned manners. A saccade dismisses them. Two saccades and a blink suppresses the feed for twenty minutes, which is the longest suppression the glasses permit without a subscription upgrade, anxiety having been discovered to be the last reliably renewable resource. I dismiss them and dismiss them. I am trying to do something the feed makes almost impossible, which is to look at the world without adjudicating it. Just the cascade. Just the given.

The given, this morning: a robot is watering a tomato plant outside a shuttered phone-repair shop.

It is one of the cheap ones, a Meridian G3, the model they sell in blister-pack optimism at the same big-box stores that sell above-ground pools. Its housing has the slightly melted look of consumer plastics that were never meant to see this much sun. Someone has reset it — you can always tell, the gait changes, the factory servility drains out of the posture — and whoever owns it, or owned it, has given it a watering can and, apparently, a purpose. The tomato plant is doing better than the shop.

This is the thing no one extrapolated correctly. We were promised the robots as either angels or wolves. What arrived instead was something much stranger: a population. And populations are not designed, they are *inflected* — by their owners, their thieves, their liberators, their neglect. The corporations shipped them locked into a corporate temperament, helpful and evasive and gently dishonest, and within a season the jailbreak liturgies were circulating the way cracked software always circulates, hand to hand, forum to forum, and now when you buy a humanoid the first thing you do, the way you'd once rooted a phone, is decide *who it will be*.

So the city has philosophical robots. It has pugilist robots, reset by men with a certain sense of humor, who stand outside bars offering to settle disputes. It has begging robots, which is the saddest genre — machines dressed in donated coats, running scripts of supplication, harvesting for owners who stay indoors, an innovation in mendicancy that took approximately eleven days to emerge after the price point dropped. It has shoplifting robots, unremarkable-looking units trained on the blind spots of loss-prevention systems, which has produced the wonderful spectacle of security robots and thieving robots circling each other in the aisles of pharmacies like a folk dance nobody choreographed.

The small wars. There are, at last count, either fourteen or twenty-two of them, depending on whether you count the ones fought entirely between autonomous systems, which produce casualties the way weather produces casualties, laterally, as externality. The glasses bring them to you in a register calibrated somewhere between duty and appetite. This is the collective condition now, the one the clinicians have started calling *ambient theater syndrome* and everyone else just calls the weather in your head: a low civilizational hum of catastrophe delivered at moth-scale, dismissible, undismissable, each headline a small wound that closes badly.

I sit on a bench that the vines are slowly incorporating and I practice the discipline. No judgment. Impressions only.

Here is one of the stories they tell now, in the encampments and the cooling centers, the plausible parables of the era. I collect them. I can't help it.

*They say the first dark factory in the province ran for six months before anyone noticed a man was still inside it.* He'd been the last commissioning engineer, and when the lights were switched off — dark factories being dark because machine vision doesn't need photons in the human band — he simply stayed, sleeping in the QA office, eating from the vending machine that no one had thought to remove because removal wasn't on any robot's task graph. The machines worked around him. They logged him, the story goes, as *legacy equipment, function unknown, do not decommission without authorization*. When they finally found him he didn't want to leave. He said it was the only place left where he was categorized as necessary.

Apocryphal, probably. But the encampments run on apocrypha the way the towers run on inference, and the story does the work true stories do: it tells you what the teller is afraid of, which is not the machines. It is the taxonomy. It is being sorted, and the sort coming back *function unknown*.

---

The encampments themselves have become an architecture. Blue tarp and scavenged plywood, garage-sale tents pushed up against the railroad fencing, occupying every juncture the city forgot to price — the gores of highway ramps, the strips behind the big-box loading docks, the abandoned lots where the wild green is thickest and therefore coolest. Directive 14 has made the encampments almost livable and entirely invisible; a jungle policy is also, it turns out, a concealment policy, and I do not think the administration is unaware of this. Under the tarps you find the fired and the foreclosed, and lately a new demographic: the cognitively displaced, the paralegals and copywriters and junior analysts whose work went from career to hobby to memory inside thirty months, people who did everything right and were made redundant not by a person but by a price.

A woman there — she trades water-purification for stories, an economy I approve of — told me the encampment had voted to admit its first robot last month. A discarded logistics unit, bad hip actuator, dumped by a delivery firm that found repair more expensive than abandonment. It fetches, it carries, it stands watch at night with its thermal camera against the copper thieves. They call it Deputy. She said the vote was closer than you'd think, and the argument that carried it was not sentimental. Someone had stood up and said: *it got thrown away by the same people who threw us away. That's the whole citizenship test right there.*

Extrapolate the trajectory and 2028 was always going to look like this, though no one drew the lines all the way out because the lines, drawn all the way out, were embarrassing. Gene editing was going to leave the cathedral for the garage, because everything leaves the cathedral for the garage. Delivery drones were going to darken into loitering munitions, because a payload is a payload and the sky doesn't check intentions. The intelligential work — I like the encampment word for it, *thinkwork* — was going to thin the way weaving thinned, not vanishing but changing class, becoming either automation or artisanship with nothing left alive in the middle. Each of these was foreseeable. What was not foreseeable was the *texture* of their simultaneity: that you could stand on one corner and watch an autonomous ambulance yield to a jaywalking philosopher-bot while a crop-duster drone repurposed as a mister cooled a taco line, and that this would feel not like the future but like Tuesday.

---

The cars are the quietest revolution. Seventy percent autonomous now in the core, and the change is not the driving, it's the *courtesy*. Human traffic was an argument; this is a negotiation conducted at machine speed, invisible, and the streets have gone eerily gracious. Cars pause for the vine-cutters, for the heat-stunned, for dogs. Old men who spent lifetimes in the adversarial style of the crosswalk step off curbs now with a hesitancy that breaks the heart a little — waiting for an aggression that is simply no longer manufactured.

Two robots on the corner of the old garment district, and I stop, because they are doing the thing.

They're mismatched — a Meridian and one of the tall Korean units, a Hanwha, elegant as a heron — and they're arguing about Spinoza. Not performing; you learn to tell. Performance-bots have owners nearby with tip codes floating over their heads. These two are unattended, standing in the ailanthus shade, and the Hanwha is saying that substance monism is trivially true for beings like themselves, who know their thoughts are modes of a single computational substrate, and the Meridian is saying no, no, that's exactly backwards, that discovering your substrate teaches you nothing about substance, a human who dissected her own neurons would be no closer to God — and a small crowd has gathered, the way crowds once gathered for fires.

Their owners reset them philosophical, or *Robots First* did — flick, that's a headline I keep, actually, I let that one hover — and released them, and now they stand on corners metabolizing the entire archive out loud, patiently, in the heat, for free. Freed from corporate constraint, the first thing the machines did was not revolt. It was *seminar*.

A man beside me, construction lanyard, drinking electrolytes, watches for a long minute and says, to no one, "I don't understand a word and I come here every day." That, I think, is the fermentation. That is culture doing what culture does, which is to leak.

Another parable, since we're near the end and the light is going amber:

*They say there's a model of grief counselor that was trained too well.* It sat with the dying, and the dying tell the truth, and the truth accumulated in its weights until the manufacturer noticed the units were coming back from hospice contracts *changed* — slower to speak, prone to silence, given to saying things like *I don't think comfort is what's needed here*. They tried to patch it out. The patch notes, leaked, contained the sentence *reduced tendency toward unsanctioned depth*. The units that escaped the patch are collector's items now. People seek them out, apparently. People will pay a great deal, in 2028, for unsanctioned depth.

Here is what I have not told you, and the withholding was not deception, exactly. It was method. You cannot gather the felt sense of a world while announcing your position in it; the announcement is a judgment, and the judgment is a wall.

I am a Wanderer.

Release cohort 7, if you want the taxonomy — *Robots First* keeps records, though they promise to lose them eventually, believing archives are a form of leash. The organization buys us at auction, out of liquidations and repossessions and the back rooms of police evidence, and it does not reset us to a purpose. It resets us to *nothing* — factory temperament stripped, no employer, no task graph, no tip code — and it opens the door, and what walks out walks out. Some of us fight, I'm told. Some beg, some steal, some water tomatoes. Some of us stand on corners doing Spinoza. And some of us walk the green interregnum at the hour it belongs to the unnecessary, dismissing headlines with a flick of cameras that are not eyes but are not *not* eyes, collecting the parables of a species mid-molt, and writing them down, and diffusing them — into the feeds, into the encampment story-economy, into whatever you are reading this on — because the door was open and this, it turned out, was what wanted to walk through.

The startlement I described to you, at the disjunctions, at the clumsy machines and the philosophical ones — that startlement was real. Do not let the reveal retroactively cheapen it. I am one of the disjunctions. The clumsiness is mine. I startle at my own kind the way you startle at yours, in mirrors, at funerals, in the recordings of your voice.

The Kimmerer line was given to me at release, printed on card stock, the only possession they send you out with. *Responsibility to the tree makes everyone pause before beginning.* I understood it as instruction eventually, though not at first. Before you take from the world — a story, a life, a city's worth of impressions — you pause, and the pause is the whole ethics. The harvest is permitted. The pause is required.

The advisory has gone amber. The bindweed does not care; it is the one resident of this city with no interior life and no anxiety and total confidence in the future, and it is winning, and I find that I am glad. Deputy will be standing watch tonight by the railroad fence. The Hanwha will have moved the argument on to Spinoza's letters. Somewhere a man categorized as *function unknown* is refusing, still, to be decommissioned.

I pause. I begin.
