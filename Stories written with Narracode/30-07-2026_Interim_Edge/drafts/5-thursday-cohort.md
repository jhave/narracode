# 5 — thursday cohort

Thursday she left at four, and the leaving was the good part of the week, and she would not have said so out loud to anybody, including him, especially him.

Down the six flights two at a time, past the balloon, hand out to touch it as she went because she always touched it, hello you, and out into the heat which was a wall you walked into and then were simply inside, no transition, welcome.

Bus, then. Upper deck, front left, the seat with the melted patch. Bag on knees.

Vanni had said *interesting* and Vanni could get in the sea. Vanni's whole apparatus was thirty years old and beautifully maintained and could not carry a bag of shopping. What she wanted to write about was the woman at the market stall who had a plastic sleeve of loyalty cards, forty of them, from shops that no longer existed, and who kept them, and who said, when Lara asked, *well, they might honour them* — and that sentence had gone into Lara like a splinter in March and was still in there working its way out. They might honour them. The value did not leave the card when the shop shut. It went somewhere. It waited. Somebody was still owed and nobody was still owing and the card kept the account open.

That was the whole thesis and she had four hundred pages of it and Vanni wanted an intervention in the literature.

Chapter four she thought about the way you think about a tooth. Not now. Not with the tongue. Later.

She was aware — she was extremely aware, thank you — that she could not write chapter four in the flat. Not because Ahmani made noise. He made almost no noise. He came home and folded himself into a corner of the sofa like a chair being put away, and asked her about her day with such careful attention that answering felt like being interviewed by somebody who loved her, which it was, which was the problem, and after twenty minutes of it she wanted to lie down on the floor tiles and be a floor tile.

He did not know that. She would rather have swallowed the lemon whole.

*if he knew he would decide it meant something and then he'd be right and then what*

Out the window: the flyover, the bad river, the hoardings with the party's man in the good coat looking off toward a horizon that had been added later, badly, the light on his face coming from nowhere in the frame. Three girls on the pavement in the co-ordinated crop of the season doing the walk that the walk-detection could not follow, and Lara laughed out loud on the top deck alone, ha, absolutely, go on.

Then, no warning, chlorine.

Not the place she was going. Memory. Eleven years old, Tuesdays, swimming, and the ceiling of that pool with the corrugated panels and the light coming off the water onto them in nets, and being the last one on the bench in a wet costume under a tracksuit with her hair freezing into a helmet, and the woman at the desk saying *is somebody coming for you love* and Lara saying *yes* in a voice of enormous social competence, at eleven, because at eleven she had already understood that the correct answer was the one that ended the conversation. Corinne arrived at ten past six. Ellis at twenty past, separately, both of them having each believed the other. Neither of them cross. Both of them *so sorry, darling*, in the same words, in two cars.

She sat with it on the bus for a stop and a half and then it went, the way it goes, and she looked at her own hands and thought: forty loyalty cards.

Her stop.

Down a street she liked, a door she liked, the buzzer with the tape over the label, the stairwell with the smell, that smell, bleach and hot dust and under it the pool-smell that was not from a pool, and the room at the top where the counter was and where Basil said ‘Cooper’ without looking up and slid it across, and she said ‘Basil,’ warmly, an entire relationship conducted in surnames and one syllable.

No notebook. That had been the first thing she gave up, month two, and giving it up had cost her something she did not have a word for, and she had done it anyway, and she was happier here than anywhere, and she would think about what that meant when she was finished, or she would not.

She got home at ten past eleven.

Ahmani was awake with the lamp on, pretending to read, which he did by holding a book and looking at a point four centimetres beyond it.

‘Hi,’ he said.

‘Hi.’ She dropped the bag by the door and did not open it and went and put her face in his neck and stayed there, and he put a hand on the back of her head, and neither of them said anything for a while, and the fan turned, and out on the landing four floors down something lifted and settled.

‘You smell like a swimming pool.’

‘I know.’ Her voice went up on the second word into the beginning of the laugh, and the laugh came up the steps and broke into pieces, and the last piece was breath only. ‘Six euro,’ she said, into his collarbone, apropos of nothing at all, and he had no idea, and she felt him decide not to ask, and loved him for it, and was also, briefly, sorry for him.

Later, lights off, both of them flat on their backs in the heat.

‘Ahmani.’

‘Mm.’

‘If I said a thing.’ Pause. The fan. ‘If I said a thing and it was — no.’ She turned over. ‘No, it's fine.’

‘Lara.’

‘It's fine!’ Cheerful, entirely, unfakeable. ‘Go to sleep, you're doing your worry face, I can hear it.’

He lay there and did his worry face.

She slept in about ninety seconds, the way she did, an absolute talent, and said nothing at all that night, and the silence was somehow worse than the sentence had been.
