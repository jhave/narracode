# 2 — high-intent, low-trust

By seven she had the table. She always had the table by seven: printouts in a fan, three colours of pen, laptop propped on a hardback about kinship she had never opened and used as furniture.

‘Lemon,’ she said, not looking up. ‘Six. Six euro. Shape of a fist.’

On the windowsill, and it was, knuckled at one end, a citrus made in the image of a threat.

‘Bought it anyway. Obviously bought it. Look at it.’ Then the laugh came, the way it came, starting at the back and going up in steps and breaking near the top into four or five pieces, each smaller, the last one breath only, and she wiped her eye with the heel of her hand and said, ‘six,’ and went back to the pen.

He cooked. Rice, tin of beans with the label half off, the last of the frozen spinach in a green brick that hissed. Extractor fan running its one note. Out the window the block opposite doing its evening, four hundred lit rectangles, a man on the third floor watering a plant with enormous care, and beneath them all the street where the bins had not been taken for eleven days because the contract had gone to arbitration and the arbitration had gone to a body that met quarterly.

‘Vanni says chapter three is interesting.’ She did the quotes with the pen. ‘Interesting means resubmit. Interesting means abstract, on a train, thumb.’

‘It's not interesting?’

‘It's *good*.’ Up went the arms. ‘It's so good, Ahmani. It's disgustingly good, I read it Tuesday and I was jealous of it.’ Laugh, cascade, gone. ‘He wants an intervention in the literature. I don't want an intervention in the literature, I want to describe what people actually do with a coupon.’

She talked while he stirred. The theory came out of her in pieces with the joints removed, and he had learned to take the pieces without asking for the joints.

‘Fetish isn't the object. Everyone goes straight to object. Fetish is the ledger — where the labour went to hide. Coat doesn't do anything. Coat is a place. There's a woman in Dhaka in the coat and we've all agreed to be polite about it.’

‘Corridor,’ she said, ten minutes later, about something else. ‘Deleuze, corridor, modulation, fine, fine. Nobody says what a corridor smells like. Wet coats. It's always wet coats.’

He told her about RS-09 because he had been carrying it since ten past nine.

She put the pen down. That was how he knew.

‘Empty field.’

‘Schema won't take empty. It takes null, null's a value. This is nothing.’

‘Sunset three times.’ She was already up, at the counter, holding the lemon without knowing she had picked it up. ‘Ahmani. Something's putting it *back*.’

‘Delph says not ours.’

‘Delph says.’ She made a sound with no vowels in it and turned to the window and said, quietly, to the glass, ‘that's a *door*,’ and he did not know whether she meant the segment or his job or the sentence, and asked, and she said ‘yes,’ and laughed, and the laugh went up the steps and broke.

After, she read. He cleared. From the sink he watched her reach and cover a page with her palm, flat, unhurried, the way you cover a hand of cards for somebody who is not accused of anything.

‘Which one?’

‘Four.’

‘Why?’

‘Not yet.’ Whole face open, entirely warm, telling him nothing whatsoever.

He dried the pot. He wanted, and was ashamed of wanting, to ask whether he was in chapter four. Whether he was material. Whether he had been, at some point in the last two years, entered into a notebook with a date on it — *informant, cohabiting, category-adjacent, unusually candid after eleven p.m.* He could see the entry. He could see the neatness of her handwriting doing it.

Later, on the sofa, the room labelled itself. Mildly, the way a headache is mild. Lemon: *aspirational produce · single-unit · affect purchase, no repeat.* The used coat over the chair back: *thrift-identified · income-suppressed · high cultural capital.* His own hands on the cushion, *manual, non-manual*, category error, and the error made him laugh out loud, once, and she said ‘what,’ and he said ‘nothing,’ and then, for less than a second, Lara resolved into a card. Face, three bullets, a lift number in the corner.

He never told her that. Not drunk, not ever, not while dying.

‘Corinne wrote,’ she said, at half eleven, from the table.

‘Your mother.’

‘Corinne wrote.’ She read it out flat. Three lines. Congratulations on the conference she had not attended, a note about the weather in the place they had moved to, an instruction to eat properly. Signed with her name. Not *mum*. Corinne.

Then her face went. No transition, none, the way weather does not transition on a screen but simply is the next frame: mouth first, then everything, and she sat with her hands in her lap while it went through her, thirty seconds, maybe forty, and he crossed the room and put his hand flat between her shoulder blades because he had learned that speaking during it made it longer.

It passed. She blew out, hard, looked up, eyes glassy and amused.

‘Eat properly,’ she said. ‘*Corinne.*’ And the laugh came back up through the wreckage of the other and he thought: this is the bravest person I have met, and also, immediately, on the same track: I don't know her at all.

In the night she said it again. She said it most nights, from far under, unhurried, addressed to somebody.

‘…not the way we said. Tell them not the way we said.’

He lay with his eyes open. Radiator ticking despite the heat, the building doing its arithmetic. Down on the fourth-floor landing, under the crushed fold of the word, the balloon lifted and settled as the street door went.
