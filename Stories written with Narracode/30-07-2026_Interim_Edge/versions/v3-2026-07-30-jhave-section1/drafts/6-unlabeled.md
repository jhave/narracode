# 6 — unlabeled

Rain came once in the third week of September, for forty minutes, and the street steamed afterwards and everybody stood in doorways smelling it, and then it went back to being what it had been.

The balloon left the landing sometime that week.

He noticed on the Tuesday, going down. Corner where the wall met the step: dust in the shape of it, a paler grey, and a curl of ribbon he had never seen because the ribbon had been underneath. On four the door stood open. Not on the chain. Open, about a hand's width, dark inside, no television, no applause, and he stood on the landing with his bag on his shoulder for longer than a person stands.

‘Hello?’

Nothing. He pulled the door to, gently, without touching the latch, which is not closing a door and is not leaving it open.

Downstairs, in the letterboxes, a delivery slip. *Attempted 08:40. Signed for by resident.* Addressed to A. MANI, flat 6.

He read the name four times. He put the slip in his pocket. He did not tell his mother.

At the office the crèche was behind glass on the ground floor, off the lobby, past the insurance marble, and he had walked by it two hundred times, and that morning he stopped.

Nine or ten of them in there. A low table, a tub of something. One kid at the glass with a hand flat on it, and the hand came off and left five fingers of fog, and the kid looked at Ahmani with the total unembarrassed attention of somebody who has not yet learned that looking costs.

He raised a hand. Chest height. It came out like a man swearing an oath badly.

The kid waved with the whole arm.

The practitioner looked up from the table. Forty, maybe, dark hair pinned, and she was not her, she was nothing like her, and Ahmani felt the disappointment arrive in his body before he had permitted the hope, and stood there with his bag on his shoulder and his lanyard turned the wrong way out, holding a wave to a child through a window in the lobby of a building where he cleared ninety items a week.

She smiled at him, politely, the way you smile at a man standing at the glass.

He went up.

CONTINUITY shipped in October. Retention held at day ninety. There was cake on twelve.

Thursday at three the invite came round again — *P. Ganz — cat-integrity walkthrough* — and Ahmani put the cursor on Decline and left it there until the mouse went to sleep.

RS-09 was at 2.1 million.

He opened it. Definition field: empty. Behaviour signature: *initiate · interrupt · return.*

He had the query written already, had had it written since August, a filter of the segment against a single device identifier, his own, and he pasted it and looked at it and then selected the whole line and deleted it.

Instead he clicked into the definition field, which had never held anything, and typed:

*people who stop to tie their shoe.*

Save. It took it. Green tick, timestamp, his initials in the audit column.

He sat with his heart going. Miko said something about the cake.

Next morning the field was empty.

Not reverted with a note, not flagged, not queried. Empty, the way it had been, in a schema that does not permit empty, and no line in the audit, and his initials gone from the column as though the hand had never been on the glass.

He did not tell Delph. He wrote it down in the notebook, the way he wrote everything down, because that was the useful part of him.

· · ·

Home. Lara at the table by seven, printouts in a fan, three colours of pen. The bag from Thursday by the door, unopened, four days now, and he had not asked and was not going to.

‘Chapter four,’ she said, without preamble, to the page.

‘Mm.’

‘Nearly.’ She looked up. Whole face. ‘Nearly, Ahmani.’

He said that was great and meant it and felt, underneath the meaning it, the floor of the fourteen months, which had become eleven, and which he had still not consented to.

He told her about the definition field. He told it badly, out of order, starting with the tick and going back to the empty, and she stopped him twice to make him do it again properly, and when he got to the morning she put both hands flat on the table.

‘It read it.’

‘It didn't read it. It's a field.’

‘Ahmani.’ She was up. ‘Something *reads the fields*.’

‘Or a job runs at four and wipes non-conforming entries.’

‘Sure,’ she said, in the voice of a person granting a point in order to keep the other one, and stood at the window with her arms folded, and then said, quietly, to the glass, ‘that's a door,’ again, and laughed once, no cascade, one note, and came back to the table.

· · ·

Dawn.

Awake before the alarm, as he was now most days, in the swivel chair with the flat grey light finding the edges of objects one at a time — the mug, the cable, the corner of her coat over the chair back — and the news tabs open because he had opened them, three of them, and the first was authorized, the second was adjacent, the third was a man in a good coat looking at a horizon that had been added later.

The dream had been the building again. Curved wall. He had the lead. He stopped.

He closed the tabs.

Behind him Lara asleep with one foot out from under, wrapped tight, mouth open, the shine at the corner of it, and he loved her so much that his chest did the medical thing, and behind that, on the same track, arriving with its own timestamp: eleven months.

There's no race, he said, out loud, in the room, at six-forty in the morning, to nobody, in the voice of a man reading a slogan off a wall.

It sounded like exactly what it was.

He said it again anyway. Then he got up and put the kettle on and stood there for the whole two minutes forty, and the water went from nothing to a hiss to that last rolling panic, and out on the landing four floors down there was only the paler grey where something had lain, and in his coat pocket the slip with the name that was almost his.
