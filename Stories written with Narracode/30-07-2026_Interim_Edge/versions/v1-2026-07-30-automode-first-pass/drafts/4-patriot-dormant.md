Somewhere in the building, allegedly, there was a room where these decisions were audited. He had seen its name on a form. He had never met anyone who had been in it, and the not-meeting had stopped seeming strange around week three, the way a smell stops.

On the left of his dashboard, a tile: *Your clearances, YTD.* Collapsed. He had never expanded it. He knew, in the general way you know your own weight, that the number was somewhere over four thousand, and the specific way he did not want to know it was the most disciplined act of his adult life. His cursor went there twice that morning and came away twice, and the second time he noticed his jaw had locked, and he unlocked it, and drank the coffee, which had gone cold and tasted of the legume.

*brand-safe · category-permissive · operator, tier 2, retention 94%*

Eleven o'clock, all-hands, floor twelve, chairs out, hampers by the door. The hampers were Cat. 2, a full wall of them, sponsor tie-in, and there was a raffle.

The VP of Growth was thirty-four and wore the good linen and had a way of pausing before nouns.

‘We've been shipping … completion,’ he said. ‘For a decade we shipped completion. And completion is a beautiful thing to ship and it is also, and I'll be honest with you, and this is going to sound harsh, it's *over*.’

Slide. One word.

**CONTINUITY™**

‘An unclosed loop,’ said the VP, ‘is a debt the mind insists on paying.’

Ahmani wrote it down. He wrote everything down, that was the useful part of him. Beside him Miko was eating from the bowl.

Growth math, then. Curves. A cohort held open, never resolved, retention at day ninety of sixty-one per cent against a benchmark of nineteen, and the mechanism was simply that nothing in the experience ever finished — no ending, no receipt, no last episode, no confirmation screen, every surface leaving one filament dangling, and the filament did the work, and the work was that people came back not out of desire but out of a kind of housekeeping.

Ahmani thought of his mother's phone face down on the table. *Concluded rather than resolved.* He wrote that down too, in the margin, and did not know whose side he had written it on.

Then a woman three rows up said, clearly, not loudly, ‘what about the sector inventory,’ and four hundred heads swivelled.

All of them. At once. That soft mass rotation of a floor turning, the chairs going, the necks, and Ahmani's own neck going with them before he could decide anything, and he sat there rotated with everybody else, looking at a woman in a green cardigan who had asked a question, her face doing what a face does when it has just discovered how many people are in a room.

She said, ‘sorry, no, it's fine, sorry.’

Heads went back.

The VP said, warmly, ‘let's take that offline,’ and the linen moved on, and the room breathed out, and somebody won the hamper, and it was Miko, who said *oh no*, delightedly, and carried it back to the desk on his hip like a toddler.

He stopped.

Row four. White hair. Serene, non-reactive, mouth doing almost nothing, eyes straight into the lens with no request in them.

Neutral. Gentle. No judgement, no disrespect. Just awareness.

He clicked through for the model release and got a spinner and then a page that said the record was held by an entity whose name was seven letters and a numeral, and a link, and the link went to the login of a system he had no credentials for and had never heard of.

He sat back. Ninth-floor hum in the soles of his shoes.

Ganz — cat-integrity walkthrough*, accepted, green, in a series with no end date. Peter Ganz had come off the org chart in March. Nobody had removed the invite. Every Thursday it came around, and every Thursday, at three, it was declined by nobody and attended by nobody, and it went on being scheduled.

Ahmani looked at it for a while. Then he opened the queue and did eleven more items before the hour turned.
