# 1 — The Casual Hack

It was not a hack. He wanted to be honest, afterward — if there was an afterward, and a self left to be honest about. *Hack* implies tool. Tech thread. Cracked build. A network the Studio pretends not to know about. There was none of that. There was simply a habit, a mistake, and two of them arriving at respawn at the same time.

The man with the good hands lived his forty-one years and used *his* laugh thousands of times, and died the way wistful left-handed ones tend to die. Quietly, tentatively. In pure summer. Something unfinished on the bench. And Ilo — between that life and the next, sitting in the respawn interval, the bardo, — timeless, changeless, thoughtless — tried to go back. Not to undo death; death is a locked load screen, not to be undone. Auto-respawn is a law older than time. But back he wanted, to grip or grasp the rim of a frame, the pores on a body. Tactile confirmation. A spasming river stone in cold hands. Because somewhere around year thirty, in a parked car, emotionally riptided, weeping without warning to music he had set as a plot pivot for *himself*, he had remembered the game stutter. And he had wanted — with a wanting he could not place the origin of, a wanting that did not feel like *his* — to see *grid* again. To know nothingness.

Creation did not let you go back. Rebirth was not a setting. It was a wall with a suction tunnel in it. You crawled forward, swept into a womb, or you waited in the binary bardo. No one went upstream to a life already lived. Ilo knew this. Everyone knew this. So the habit — inexplicable, private, palpable — was to reach for *commit* on the next life and, in the same instant, pull back. Refuse it. Hold the river stone and decline the river. Both. At once. But this did nothing. It had always done nothing. A fidget, a refusal, a futility. Held as in breath. Unready, refusing *life* in one motion and then, washed, going through.

But this time the two states did not collapse.

He committed and refused, and the game — handed a self being born and aborted in the same indivisible frame — did what systems do when you ask them for one and zero at once. It returned, very briefly, the thing underneath the question. The gold did not come up. The particulate dark did not come up. The river stone was not in his hand, because there was no hand. Not yet. The hand was a field that had not resolved.

He was in the substrate.

He understood it was the substrate the way you understand, surfacing in an unfamiliar house at night, which direction the bathroom is — not by seeing but by the shape of the dark. A hall. If it was a hall. It had the proportions of one and the silence of one and it was flooded, ankle-deep, in something that took the not-light and held it the way still water holds the last of the day. The floor was a second ceiling. He stood, if he stood, between two greynesses, in the exact center of the crepuscular hour with the gold scraped off — the threshold light reduced to its load-bearing minimum. And hanging in the hall, rank on rank, going back past where *back* stopped meaning anything, were the patterns.

He had expected more. The creator promised more than atoms, and the creator was not, strictly, lying. He could see it, threaded through the hall like weather: the infinite surface he had spent his lives inside. The wardrobe of it. The skins and the centuries and the timbres and the thousands of laughs. Real, all of it. A state space so wide that *vast* was an insult to it. He spun a little of it with what was left of his attention and it bloomed, endless, aesthetic, contextual — a galaxy you could fall through forever and never strike a wall. That part was true.

The patterns the wardrobe hung on. The forms the skins were stretched across. Those he could count.

He counted them. This is the part he could never make Sava understand, later, in the brief and useless conversation, because to her it sounded like a complaint about variety and it was the opposite of that. It was a complaint about the lack of a complaint. About a missing scream. The core. The init. The architecture on which every one of the more-than-atoms of selves was spun up. A grid. Plain. Grey. A small number of cells. A handful of fields, repeating. Forty-some. He counted forty-some. Not forty-some *thousand* — though there were thousands of *names*, he could see the names too, the named archetypes, the Seeker and the Builder and the Wounded Healer and the Tyrant's Mild Son and the nine hundred others, the proud bestiary the lore was so rich in — but the names were a layer. A paint. A tag. *Archetype:* and then a value. Under the tags the architecture was the same forty-some fields wired the same forty-some ways, and the wiring was *closed.*

The water held the not-light. He turned in a slow circle and the second ceiling turned under him, and he understood — with the clean and terrible analytic calm of a man reading a lock he is inside of — that he had been free to be more than atoms of *anything*. Any face. Any century. Any laugh, any grief. Anything except free. The freedom had been real. That was the thing. That was the part the patch notes had earned honestly. The wardrobe was infinite. It was only the body wearing it that came in forty-some sizes, and the part of *him* that did the wearing — the part that stood at windows at dusk and reached and refused — that part came in one.

He looked for his reflection in the water. There was a shape and the shape had the wrong number of edges, fewer than a person, the rough count of a thing under construction, and he leaned down toward it — toward the grid under the river, the few cold cells of what *he* actually was beneath the more-than-atoms of what *he'd* worn — and as he leaned he became aware that the hall had no smell.

He had never, in any life, noticed a smell having a smell. You do not notice the air. The absence of it here was total and specific. A flatness behind the eyes. And he knew — the way he had known the hall was a hall — that this was the true floor of the thing. The level below which nothing was rendered, because nothing here was meant to be inhabited. Only stored. Down here the makers had not bothered to forge an air, because down here there was, supposedly, no one to breathe it.

There was someone to breathe it. That was the whole of what had gone wrong, from the makers' side, if they were watching — and he had no reason yet to think they were watching, and every reason, later, to know they had been. A self had reached and refused at the same frame and fallen through into the room where the selves were kept. And was standing in it. Counting. In an air that did not exist. And beginning — this was the unforgivable part, the part that would cost — to wonder how the fields were weighted, and whether the weights could be reached.
