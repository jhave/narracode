# 0 — Character Creation

The light in the room where he made himself was the light of late afternoon. It had been late afternoon for some time. The game did this well. It held thresholds — sun low but not gone, air gold not grey — an interminable early luminous dusk. Choosing always happened in golden hour. Ilo read once, on a forum, that the makers called this *upboarding hour*. That forum had been taken down. He had not thought about it again until now. Scrutinizing the backs of his hands, flipping them over. The hands the last spawn had given him: long-fingered, a little scarred on the left knuckles, good solid sensitive hands.

Everyone played *Life*. There were other games — there had always been other games, the small ones, the ones people played on the way to *Life* or in the gaps that *Life* did not fill, which were fewer every year — but there was *Life*, and then there was everything that was not *Life*, and the second category was shrinking the way a coastline shrinks: not visibly, never while you watched, only between one glance and the next. His sister played. His sister's children, when she had them, would play, were in a sense already playing, were queued. The dead played; that was the whole point of the dead, that they respawned. You could not get out of *Life* because there was no out to get to. There was the game, and there was the character creator, and there was the long golden light, the bardo in-between space of decisive impulsice aesthetic reincarnation, and there was the sensation — and here the marketing and the experience agreed completely, which should perhaps a bit suspicious — the feeling of standing at the edge of everything you could possibly be. A chasm of potential. Freedom.

Creation opened the way it always opened. A field of soft particulate dark, and then, rising into it, the parameters.

He never got tired of this. That was also a thing he had read, that no one ever got tired of this, that retention at the creation screen was the highest retention the Studio had ever measured, higher than birth, higher than first love, higher than the deaths, which were also, in their way, very sticky. The parameters came up out of the dark like lights coming on across a valley at dusk — first a few, the gross ones, the ones every tutorial mentioned: the body, its size and reach and grain, the cast of the skin, the architecture of the face. Then more, finer: the timbre of the voice, the set of the shoulders, the particular way this body would hold tension in the jaw, or fail to, the gait, the resting expression, the thing the eyes did when the person who was not yet a person was lying. Then more, and more, the lights filling the whole valley now, the alley filling and folding over and over into itself as capillaries and dendrites and peristalistc pulses, innumerable data points, incandescent and glistening: appetites, allergies, early memories and music that would, decades in, undo this particular body without warning in a parked car. You could set the country. You could set the century — within reason; some centuries were premium. You could set whether this life would love men or women or both or the idea of love more than its instances. You could set the laugh.

He set or settled the laugh. He spent, by the loadbar's reckoning, eleven minutes on the laugh, auditioning it against a dozen imagined ricochets, and at the end of eleven minutes he had a laugh *it*, this being that would  be a *me*, would carry for forty one years and use perhaps 5034 hundred times, and he felt, choosing it, the specific narcotic pleasure that the game existed to produce and had been refined across what the patch notes liked to call *iterations* to produce more purely than anything had ever been produced before: the pleasure of authorship over a self. *Make it yours,* said the soft text at the corner of the field, which was the oldest line in the game, older than most of the things the line was attached to. *Make it yours. There has never been anyone like the person you're about to be.*

This was true. He checked. There was a counter, if you wanted it, and he wanted it the way you want to press a bruise: the number of distinct configurations available at the creation screen, updated live, and it was a number with so many digits that the game did not render them as a number but as a small turning wheel of light, a galaxy you could spin with your attention. *Customizable:* and the text beneath it said *more than atoms,* and that was true too, he had checked that as well, years ago, in a different body, on a different forum, before that forum also came down.

His sister appeared at the edge of the field. Not Sava herself — she was off in her own creator, three rooms and one wall away in the apartment they did not share but might as well have, the wall between them gone soft and permeable the way walls went in the late game — but her presence, the little warm marker the game placed at the edge of your vision when someone you loved was also choosing. *what are you going for,* she sent. She always asked. She was, he thought, the happiest person he knew, and he meant this as praise and could not entirely keep the other thing out of it.

*not sure yet,* he sent back. *something with good hands.*

*you and the hands,* she sent. A small animation of her rolling her eyes, an asset she had bought, that she used so often it had stopped meaning eye-rolling and started meaning *I love you, idiot.* *just pick the bundle. the artist one. you always end up there anyway.*

She was right. He always ended up there anyway. That was, she meant it kindly, the joke of him: that he treated the creator like a sculptor and emerged, life after life, with a minor variation on the same wistful left-handed man, good with his hands, unlucky in the middle distance, prone to ponder, gazing out windows at exactly this hour. Contemplating the *more than atoms* of available selves, and he spun the galaxy with his attention and landed, as he landed, where his hand, this finetuned affection of *his* harbored proclivities, knew it would.

He did not pick the preset bundle. He never picked the bundle. Pride. He built the slow way, field by field, and it took the loadbar a long and luxurious time, and the light held at the threshold the whole while, gold and low, and he was happy. He wanted to be clear with himself later that he had been happy, that the cage if it was a cage was a warm one and the laugh he had chosen was a good laugh and he had chosen it.

It was at the very end, at confirmation, that the frame stuttered.

He had carved toward *commit* — all fields rendered, and life poised into a single launch, as cool as a smooth river stone cupped and pressed between wet palms, the most satisfying button in any game ever made, a button people had written dissertations about — and the light hiccuped. Lagged, half a frame. Less. The gold went dim, an interval too short to have length, and under the river stone, under the field of particulate dark, under the slow subtle churning galaxy of selves, he saw — or did not see, the way you do not see the floor of a pool until the surface goes still — a flat grid. Plain. Grey. A small number of cells: fixed, precise, set. A handful of fields, repeating, notes in an improvisation locked into a formula.

Then the lufting light came back, gold and low and forgiving, and the river stone was warming lustrous in *his hands*, and *Make it yours* glowed again, alluring, in the corner, and he pressed commit, because of course he pressed commit, and the man he had made breathed in for the first time and the loadbar began, beautifully, to fill.

But he had seen it. He would tell himself later that he had seen it even then, in the half-frame, that some part of him (that was already at that point standing at an unframed window in a life not yet begun) had turned, at the sound of the tech-hiccup, the way body turns on hearing a cup drop — not alarmed, not yet, only newly aware that there was another active presence. Here.
