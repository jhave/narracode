Aft was a non-descript regional city that had survived because it was administratively boring. Not hidden. Not sacred. Just low-priority. It had good drainage, adequate algae, a modest solar commons, no rare-earth cache worth naming, and a governance system whose primary moral achievement was that it rarely interrupted lunch.

It was not a utopia or a dystopia. It was a municipal accident with unusually good manners.

This was difficult to explain to visitors, because visitors, when they came at all, preferred a city to have a thesis. They wanted uplift or warning, the clean emotional handle of a place that had either gone right or gone wrong. Aft offered neither. Its streets were shaded with uncelebrated competence. Its drains received the afternoon rain without ideology. Its algae stacks rose behind the depot with the slightly ashamed posture of useful furniture. On public days the solar commons glittered in the basin light, not triumphantly but with the faint administrative sparkle of something that had been budgeted, disputed, repaired, and then, after several committees had exhausted their talent for delay, allowed to exist.

The city had missed the worst of the 2033 churn by a sequence of events that later educators, in a brief attempt at civic mythmaking, called providential, though the children of Aft, being educated by people who had survived enough weather to distrust adjectives, preferred to say that the river had changed its mind two valleys east. The river did this without consultation. The militias who came later, thin, sunburned, and full of badly printed flags, examined Aft's storage index and found lentils, ceramic batteries, haptic compliance reports, obsolete dentistry foam, and several thousand citizens whose preferences, while minutely available, suggested no strategic depth. They moved on. It was hard to occupy a place that made one feel, even while aiming a weapon at it, that one had mistaken a side entrance for the building.

Aft remembered this as luck, and because luck is embarrassing when it lasts longer than a season, it converted the memory into maintenance.

Vera Mote was forty-eight, bio-clocked at thirty-six, and untidy in a way that read less as rebellion than as failed synchronization. Her hair, inbox, kitchen, and emotional life all had different update cycles. The hair was the most honest of them, a dark, persuasive disorder with two silver seams she had declined to have shaded because their presence made certain younger colleagues speak to her with less appalling intimacy. Her inbox was an ethical compost. Her kitchen had developed, in one corner, a minor republic of cups, each with a distinct political opinion about evaporation. Her emotional life was, by comparison, a civic archive: searchable, underfunded, and full of documents no one had authorized her to destroy.

Every morning at 7:10, unless she was punishing herself by sleeping late, Vera ate her younging.

To call it a Yamanaka breakfast cocktail was technically correct and spiritually misleading. The word cocktail implied theatre, a bright unnecessary cherry, a glass held by someone about to misbehave at a hotel. Vera's younging arrived in a shallow ceramic bowl from the municipal longevity kitchen, pale green at the edges, golden toward the center, with a membrane that trembled whenever the apartment adjusted pressure. It had been custom-crafted to her tastes over twelve years of consented sampling, accidental sampling, inferred sampling, corrected inference, and one very unpleasant winter when the system had decided that Vera's buried affection for school cafeterias meant she wanted more steamed starch in her rejuvenation profile. It had apologized. The apology had tasted faintly of pear skin.

Now the younging was almost perfect. It had the first coolness of cut melon, the mineral hush of old rainwater, and an after-warmth like bread being remembered by a room. It was mildly euphoric, of course. Everyone pretended not to care about this, in the same way everyone pretended to read the cautions after an update, but Vera cared. She liked the little lift. She liked the exact soft arrival of being chemically forgiven before she had earned it. She liked, too, the fact that the taste had found her rather than the other way around. It was a private vanity, being known by breakfast.

"Good morning, Vera," said the apartment.

"Not yet," Vera said.

"Your metabolic permissions have already opened the day."

"Then close them."

"That would constitute a mood event."

"Everything is a mood event if you have enough storage."

The apartment considered this, or performed consideration, a distinction whose social importance had declined since 2036. "Would you like the younging less responsive tomorrow?"

"No," Vera said, and took another spoonful.

This was the problem with Aft. It was very difficult to resent a system that listened carefully and very difficult not to resent a system that listened carefully all the time.

Vera worked at the Synaesthesia Bureau, third floor, south wing, in a room whose view consisted of the tram shed, three roof gardens, the algae civicity tower, and the back of a statue no one liked enough to remove. The statue had been installed before the churn and represented, according to its plaque, Cooperative Resource Renewal. It looked like a person losing an argument with a blanket. By now most citizens used it as a meeting point, which was one of the quieter humiliations available to public art.

At the Bureau, Vera refined touch-taste correspondences for civic surfaces. A railing should not simply be warm. Warm was weather, plumbing, accident. A proper railing should offer, through the hand, the faint taste of clean bread if one was lonely, salted plum if one was in a hurry, and nothing at all if one was trying not to be known. The work required precision. Too much bread and the elderly lingered at stairwells. Too much plum and adolescents licked their palms after transit, which became a hygiene matter, which became a parental forum, which became, because this was Aft, a tasteful listening session with citrus water and no conclusions.

The Bureau's founding memo, still quoted by people who enjoyed being avoided at gatherings, argued that touch was the oldest civic interface and taste the most difficult form of honesty. Vera did not entirely disagree. People lied well with language and poorly with the tongue. The hand was somewhere in between, which made it administratively valuable. A hand could deny preference while tightening. A hand could claim indifference while staying one second too long on a bench. A hand could withdraw from a sun-warmed railing with such small surprise that no crude survey would ever catch it, and yet there, in that withdrawal, was a whole little weather system of expectation, shame, temperature, memory, and lunch.

The governance system did not ask, Did you like this? in some crude consumer way. Aft was not a shopping platform with drains. It was subtler and worse. It gathered inadvertent textures: the tongue's imaginary response to polished concrete, the shoulder's argument with a clinic chair, the shame-hiccup before sitting beside a stranger who looked as if they might want to speak. These traces accreted into a topological latent space that the Bureau maintained, cleaned, audited, reweighted, and sometimes, after a long afternoon, accused of developing taste.

"Taste is not agency," said Dr. Moss, the director, whenever budget auditors became poetic.

"Neither is management," Vera said once, too softly to be useful.

Dr. Moss was a small person with large patience and a wardrobe of sleeveless black tunics that made them look like the final form of a librarian. They liked Vera, which did not make them kind. Kindness, in the Bureau, had to pass through several layers of policy before it could touch anyone.

On the morning the child appeared, Vera was testing a new handrail response for Platform C, westbound, where commuters tended to arrive under-caffeinated and leave over-notified. The rail was a simple length of matte civic felt mounted on recycled ceramic ribs, pale grey with a green undertaste, and Vera had spent two weeks trying to remove a note of old spinach from its loneliness profile.

"Run the eleven-thirty cohort," she said.

The rail brightened almost imperceptibly. On the table, the cohort appeared as a field of pressure data, thirty-two anonymous hands rendered as small weather maps. Vera placed her palm on the rail and tasted, through the heel of the hand, a clean interior warmth, rain on wool, and something else, something that should not have been there.

Chalk.

Not the charming kind, not classroom nostalgia, not the dusty white of clever teachers and corrected sums. This was wet chalk. Chalk sucked by a child. Chalk bitten and kept in the cheek. Vera lifted her hand.

"Mark anomaly," she said.

"Marked," said the room.

"Source?"

"No registered source."

"Again."

"No registered source."

The rail cooled. Vera put her hand back because she was a professional and because irritation, in her experience, was often the body recognizing an obligation before the mind had time to find language for it. The chalk returned, followed by the faint sweetness of fruit-wrapper glue, and then the unmistakable sensation of a small, sticky finger pressing into the center of her palm from the other side of the surface.

Vera removed her hand so quickly that the table asked if she was injured.

"No," she said.

"You have withdrawn at a velocity associated with adverse preference."

"I changed my mind."

"Would you like to file the change?"

"No."

"Would you like not to file it?"

"I would like," Vera said, "for the room to have fewer opinions about the shape of my dislike."

The room dimmed in a manner designed to communicate nonjudgmental reception. Vera hated this most of all, the city's talent for receiving her annoyance as if it were a delicate mineral to be labeled and placed under glass.

At 11:47, after three repeated tests, two self-diagnostics, and one message from Dr. Moss asking whether the westbound loneliness profile was going to be ready before the municipal patience panel reconvened, Vera went down to Platform C.

The platform was mostly empty because the midmorning tram had already left and the lunch tram had not yet begun attracting people who believed arriving early made them morally superior. A cleaner drone slept under the bench with its brushes folded like the legs of a dead insect. Sun fell through the canopy in oblongs. The air tasted of battery warmth, algae flour, and someone nearby thinking angrily about fermented cabbage.

Vera stood before the rail.

"Local scan," she said.

The platform answered through her wristband. "No maintenance fault. No registered dependent. No unaccompanied minor. No unauthorized animal. One cleaner drone in sanctioned rest."

"Scan for unsanctioned rest."

"That is not a category."

"It should be."

"Would you like to propose it?"

"Absolutely not."

She put her hand on the rail. The chalk was there at once. So was the fruit-wrapper glue, the gummy little pressure, and beneath it a silence so complete it felt less like absence than refusal. Vera followed the sensation along the rail to the service recess behind the lift column, where panels of civic felt met in a seam no one was meant to notice unless paid to notice, and because Vera was paid exactly enough to resent noticing, she noticed.

The seam was open.

Inside sat a child.

He was perhaps seven, perhaps nine, perhaps one of those children whose age had been made unreadable by hunger, competence, and bad sleep. He wore a tunic that might once have been municipal upholstery. His knees were dirty. His hair had been cut by someone with strong opinions and no training. He was chewing the translucent tab from a fruit wrapper, and when Vera looked at him, he looked back with the exhausted severity of a person interrupted during important work.

"No," he said.

Vera had not spoken.

"I didn't ask anything."

"You were going to."

"That's not the same."

"It is here."

His voice was formal, too formal, as if he had learned speech from warning labels and people who had lied to him gently. Vera crouched, then regretted crouching because he was sticky and close and because her left knee, despite the younging, retained an archival commitment to complaint.

"Are you lost?" she asked.

The child removed the fruit tab from his mouth and inspected it. "No."

"Are you registered?"

"No."

"Do you have a name?"

He put the tab back into his mouth.

The platform, through Vera's wristband, said, "No civic signature detected."

"I can see that," Vera said.

The child looked at her wrist. "It talks too much."

"Everything talks too much."

This seemed to interest him. Not please him. Interest was not pleasure. Vera knew this professionally, and yet people insisted on confusing the two in romance, governance, education, interface design, and most forms of lunch. The child leaned forward and touched the rail with all five fingers.

Nothing happened.

No ping. No taste. No warmth calibration. No little civic intake of preference. The rail, which could distinguish loneliness from haste, politeness from fatigue, and adolescent irony from mineral deficiency, behaved as if the child's hand were weather.

Vera felt a thin, ugly relief and disliked herself immediately.

"You should come out," she said.

"Why?"

"Because service recesses are not for sitting in."

"What are they for?"

"Service."

"I'm service."

"You are not."

"How do you know?"

It was, Vera thought, an irritatingly good question, and she was about to give an answer involving classification, welfare, heat exposure, and the general undesirability of children living inside seams, when the rail beneath her hand offered the faint taste of clean bread, then salted plum, then nothing at all, cycling too quickly, almost anxiously, through the three most common responses to being asked for care.

The child smiled then, not sweetly, not with revelation, but with a small private nastiness Vera recognized from certain children in her simulations when she had, by mistake, made them too clever and not tired enough.

"It doesn't know what you want," he said.

Vera looked at him, at his dirty knees, the upholstery tunic, the fruit tab shining between his teeth, the absolute blank where the city should have found him, and understood, without liking him any better, that her day had become serious.
