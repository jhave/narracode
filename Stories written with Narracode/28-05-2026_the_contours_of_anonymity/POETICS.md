# POETICS

## Display title

The Contours of Anonymity

## Subtitle

A Late Afternoon

## Display synopsis

In a cluttered apartment in an unnamed city, three aging companions share an afternoon. She lives there; the other two—her former lover and a man she merely tolerates—have come for tea. They do not talk about the banks, the grocery stores, or the seventeen years of quiet extraction. They talk about music (lyrics can convey what emotions they can no longer express directly), the weather in remote regions of the globe (a code for other political systems), climate change (the collapse of authenticity in politics, science as impotent in the face of capitalism), the absurdity of the art market (a code for how they generate income), caring for houseplants (a metaphor for other current anti-capitalist projects but also just houseplants, just climate change, innocent chatter about the weather, though heat can also be interpreted in the hippie sense, it is also just another heat wave washing thru the city, drenched teenagers hopping in and out of fountains, gig workers cycling into heat stroke, elderly folks like themselves living without ac, vulnerable...), and the way the market down the street organizes the lives of the people who work and buy there (and why another franchise in another region visited long ago was "liberated" by their activities). Underneath the tea and the innocuous chatting lies an unspoken history: the heavy weight of their shared capture by time, and the certainty that they are already caught, even if the door has not yet been kicked in.

## Project

A fictionalized, highly atmospheric account of a trio of aging radical activists (modeled after Daniela Klette, Ernst-Volker Staub, and Burkhard Garweg) living outside the legal apparatus for decades as fugitives. 

The piece is set in Daniela Klette's cluttered apartment in Berlin in 2023. No names are revealed. No country is specified. No years are named. The entire narrative relies on the flash of interiority mingled with dialogue, using no quotation marks, drawing on the stylistic register of Sally Rooney and the stream-of-consciousness detail of Virginia Woolf. The emotional temperature is governed by the austerity and profound reserve of Kazuo Ishiguro’s *Never Let Me Go*, shot through with the heavy, circular inevitability of László Krasznahorkai’s characters who are reconciled to their own quiet capture.

## Style and Architecture

### 1. Rooney-Woolf Dialogue Blending
Dialogue must not be set off by quotation marks. It should flow directly from the character's sensory experience, integrated seamlessly into the stream of thought:
- *Example:* She looked at the blue rim of the cup and said it was getting colder earlier this year. He didn't answer right away but she knew he had heard because his fingers tightened on the handles. It is, he said.

### 2. Ishiguro Austerity & Reserve
Characters speak with extreme control and emotional reserve. Tensions are felt through small gaps, pauses, physical gestures, and domestic duties (making tea, slicing bread, adjusting a draft blocker). The deep love between her and the former lover, and the mild friction with the collaborator she merely tolerates, must be shown entirely through spatial distance and subtle shifts in attention.

### 3. Krasznahorkai Inevitability
A heavy, atmospheric reconciliation to their fate. They are already captured—not by the authorities yet, but by the weight of their own history, the clutter of their rooms, and the silence they must maintain. The fear of being heard is a physical presence in the room; they speak softly, they do not mention their operations, and their eyes return frequently to the street or the wall.

### 4. Ideological Subtext (The Conversation)
The discussion of patriarchy and capitalism should not be a strident political manifesto. Instead, it must emerge as a relaxed, weary, and deeply familiar dialogue between old friends. They have lived these arguments; the critique is as comfortable and worn as an old wool sweater. They speak of the market down the street, the price of butter, and the way the state demands their names just to let them exist.

### 5. The Epilogue
The story closes with an epilogue focusing on her thoughts in a cell (following the 2024 arrest, though the year remains unspecified,post-trail and sentencing, finally caught yet released from waiting for it). She is interrogated repeatedly. Does she know where they are? She does not. The absolute insulation of their system—their code of anonymity—was designed to protect them from each other's futures. Elsewhere, two anonymous old men are settling peacefully into their beds under different names.

## Absolute Stylistic Constraints

1. **No Quotation Marks:** Never use quotation marks for dialogue.
2. **No Direct Identifiers:** Never mention Germany, the RAF, the Red Brigade, Daniela Klette, Berlin, or the names of the collaborators. 
3. **No Operational Language:** The words *heist, robbery, bank, gun, weapon, bomb, terrorist, militant, fugitive, police, police officer, arrest, DNA, toothbrush* are strictly forbidden in the main text of the story (with the exceptions of *toothbrush* and *DNA* in the epilogue to ground the factual connection).
4. **Concrete Presence:** Ground the scene in tactile details—the hum of the old refrigerator, the weight of the silver-plated teaspoons, the texture of the cash stuffed into empty biscuit tins, the smell of damp wool.
5. **Absolute Immersiveness:** Avoid phrases that are "this NOT that" ("it was a house not a home", "it was a meal not a dinner"). Avoid overuse of "the" in front of every noun.

## Characters

- **She (The Host):** Sincere, aging, carrying a quiet authority. She has lived in this apartment for years under a name that belongs to someone else. Her interiority is sharp, sensory, and protective. She is the one who will eventually be taken.
- **He (The Former Lover):** A quiet, gentle, yet perturbed lonely, she knows his depression, loneliness and rage, but these are muted. The connection between them is old, soft, and somatic. A look or a shared cup of tea carries decades of unspoken weight.
- **The Other (The Collaborator she tolerates):** Prickly, slightly detached, more concerned with the drafts under the door or the noise from the neighbors. He is a professional, but there is no warmth between them—only the shared, unbreakable pact of their survival.

## Attic & Clutter (The Setting)
The apartment is crowded with the debris of decades:
- Books, CDs, and old magazines.
- Biscuit tins containing stacks of worn banknotes, untouched, regarded merely as paper weight.
- The single toothbrush in the plastic cup. Another in a drawer, sealed in a plastic bag.
- The wigs in the cardboard boxes in the closet.
- The weapons stash, the fake bazooka. (She thinks about it once, perhaps the readers imagines it, but the author doesn't explicitly state what it is just suggests contours. Much of the inner dialog is like that it explores contours that become palpable as the setting of their life: set within struggle against paradigms and complicity.)
- The silence that fills the gaps between their sentences.
