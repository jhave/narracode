## 21. Amsterdam, November

I had told my mother I had a friend in Amsterdam. It took nineteen days to make the sentence true.

Wim had the back room and did not want money for it, which worried me until I understood that he wanted an audience. He printed art books on the ground floor. He played clarinet badly at seven in the morning and apologised at eight, and by December I could tell from the apology how the printing had gone.

Manon's cousin turned out to be a woman named Roos who ran a kitchen on the Jan Evertsenstraat and hired me on a Tuesday because a Portuguese boy had gone home. Nine euros ninety an hour, off the books, plus what was left at the end of service. I had thought Europe would be cheaper. Europe was not cheaper.

Sanne found me, which I had not expected and had been arranging in my head for two months. Anouk posted a photograph of the Jetta being returned and I recognised the parking structure.

She came to the kitchen at eleven at night and waited outside because the door sticks.

We went to hers. It was good and it was nothing like Nipigon, and neither of us said so, which I took as a kindness on both sides. She was moving to Rotterdam in the spring for a job in logistics. She said the word logistics the way you say a diagnosis you have made peace with.

At three my phone was at four percent and I lay in a stranger's bed in a city I had lied about, fully warm, entirely lost.

## 22. Ghent, then a bus, January

By now everybody knows that the cheapest way across Europe in winter is the night bus, and everybody is right about why.

Twenty-six hours Amsterdam–Ghent–Cologne–Hamburg, a toilet that was locked at Bremen and stayed locked, a Bulgarian driver who took corners like a man being timed. In Ghent I stayed four nights with a woman Roos knew, in a flat above a bicycle repair, and looked at a painting in a cathedral for as long as they would let me and came out onto a wet square feeling nothing I could use.

The man in seat 14C was Slovak, a welder going to a job in Esbjerg, and we talked from midnight to Hamburg about his daughter's asthma. He was handsome in a way that made no argument. Nothing charged either way. At Hamburg he gave me half a bar of chocolate and shook my hand like a colleague.

I had a job in Bergen I had lied to get. The lie was that I had done two years of front of house in Montreal. The lie would hold for four months, which is longer than most things.

## 23. Bergen, February

Rain on a skylight still puts me in Bergen.

It rained twenty-one days of my first month and the Norwegians were pleased about it in a manner I did not immediately understand, which is that the rain is theirs and the weather is a thing they own and you do not.

Blaž Rozman was from Maribor and was in the fourth year of a doctorate at the Centre for Digital Narrative, which is a real institute on a hill with good coffee and a view of a container port.

His subject was the sexual orientation of characters who do not exist. He had a corpus of three thousand four hundred non-player characters, avatars and AI influencers, and he was coding each one for what it was permitted to want. Most were permitted nothing. A number were permitted to want a person of the opposite sex, briefly, in a cutscene. Eleven had been given a same-sex partner and then patched.

He was not in charge of his own dataset. A licence sat with a company in Malmö and he had to ask them, in writing, before he could publish a table.

I asked him at the third or fourth dinner whether it depressed him and he said no, it clarified him, which is a Slovenian answer.

He had bad posture, beautiful hands, and a laugh he did not use on strangers. He cooked one dish extremely well and everything else at a level I would describe as brave. He asked me questions and then remembered the answers a week later, which no one had done for me in a very long time.

I fell in love with him in the second week of March, at a bus stop on Nygårdsgaten, while he was explaining something about metadata. I would like to report that the moment had dignity.

## 24. Bergen, March

You are supposed to go twice a year.

I had not been since Montreal and something on the lower left had begun to speak to me at night, and in Norway an adult pays for her own mouth. Terje's practice was on the second floor above a shoe shop, and the receptionist quoted me one thousand nine hundred kroner for the examination in a tone that suggested she had watched people leave.

He was fifty-one, married, two daughters at the Katedralskole, a wedding ring on the tray beside the instruments because of the gloves.

Here is what a dentist has. He has your whole face for forty minutes. He has a lamp and a reason. His hands are already at your mouth and he is talking the entire time in a low voice about nothing, about the ferry, about your accent, and you cannot answer, and being unable to answer turns out to be a condition I should have known more about myself.

At the second appointment he asked where I was from and I said Montreal and he said, ah, and did not follow it up, and I decided on the spot that I wanted him.

It took eleven days. His office on a Thursday afternoon, which he did not seem to have arranged and had entirely arranged.

I was not troubled about the wife. I want to be accurate: I thought about her precisely twice, both times in the stairwell, both times briefly.

That month I was in love with Blaž and going to Terje on Thursdays and working four dinner services a week, and the arithmetic of it did not distress me. What distressed me was that I had begun to be tired in a new way. Not sleep. I was available to everybody, all day, in two languages, at a high standard, and I could feel the standard slipping in small places, a name forgotten, a message read and not answered for a week.

Blaž said, you have gone quiet.

I said I was tired.

He said, from what, kindly, and I could not answer that either.

## 25. Florø, April

A year before, almost to the week, I had stood in a chain pharmacy in southern Alberta while a stranger rang through a fifty-two dollar box, and I had thought that was as much as a Sunday could hold.

I took the express boat north on the Friday. Four and a half hours up the coast with the islands going by, low and bare and the colour of a wet dog, oil platforms out west somewhere in the murk. Sixty-one degrees north. The boat smelled of diesel and instant coffee.

Florø is a small town that services larger things. I had gone because Blaž was at a conference in Tampere and because I wanted to be somewhere nobody could reach in under four hours.

The Bunnpris was on the way back from the harbour and I went in for bread and a bag of the salty liquorice I had learned to eat.

A woman went down in the aisle with the tinned fish.

She was about sixty, in a red anorak, and she went down without putting a hand out, which is the part I keep. Her basket came apart on the floor. Then her back arched and her heels went at the linoleum and a sound came out of her that had nothing to do with a person, and it went on, and it went on longer than I would have believed, and her handbag was still on her arm.

Two men took charge. One went to his knees and got a folded coat under her head and turned her, and the other stood up and shouted for the manager in a completely level voice.

A girl in a Bunnpris polo came at a run with a phone already at her ear.

The rest of us stood at the ends of the aisle.

I did nothing. There was nothing to do; four people were doing it; and I want to be exact about my own conduct: I did not step forward, and I did not look away, and I noticed that her shoes were good.

The ambulance took nine minutes. When they lifted her she had come back partway and was saying a word I did not have.

I bought the bread. I did not buy the liquorice. I have not been able to explain the liquorice to myself since.

## 26. Bergen, April

Everyone says the shock is at the time. The shock was at the time and then it was for eleven days afterwards.

I got back on the Sunday and did not sleep properly again in that country. Not nightmares. I would be prepping mise en place at four in the afternoon and my hands would be somewhere else, and I would be standing in the aisle with the tinned fish, and a woman I had never met and would never meet would go down again without putting a hand out.

Everything had a charge on it. Blaž's hand on the back of my neck. Terje's stairwell. The clarinet, eleven hundred kilometres away, at seven in the morning. A man laughing on the Fløibanen. All of it came in at the same volume, which is not a way you can live.

On the Wednesday Blaž made the one dish and asked me, twice, what had happened up there, and I told him accurately, and he was good about it, and it did not touch the thing at all.

On the Thursday I did not go to Terje. I did not message him either, which was the only genuinely cheap act of that spring.

On the Friday I booked a flight to Schiphol for the Sunday. Norwegian, 06:55, and the second bag was five hundred and ninety kroner, which I paid without the usual argument with myself.

I told Roos I was coming back and she said the Portuguese boy had returned and left again.

Blaž drove me to Flesland at half past four in the morning and got out of the car, which he did not have to do, and held onto me in the drop-off lane for longer than the sign permits. He said, this is not the end of it. I said no. Both of us were guessing.

The plane went up through the cloud and came out on top into a light I had not seen in three months, and I put my head against the window, and I did not sleep this time, and somewhere over the North Sea I made the first honest decision of the year, which was to eat the breakfast.
