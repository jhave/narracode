## 21. Amsterdam, November

I had told my mother I had a friend in Amsterdam. It took nineteen days to make the sentence true.

Wim had the back room and would not take money for it. This worried me until I understood he wanted an audience. He printed art books on the ground floor. He played clarinet badly at seven in the morning and apologised at eight. By December I could tell from the apology how the printing had gone.

Manon's cousin was a woman named Roos with a kitchen on the Jan Evertsenstraat. She hired me on a Tuesday because a Portuguese boy had gone home. Nine euros ninety, off the books, plus what was left at the end of service. I had assumed Europe would be cheaper. Europe was not cheaper.

Sanne found me, which I had not expected and had been arranging in my head for weeks. Anouk posted a photograph of the Jetta being returned. I recognised the parking structure.

She came to the kitchen after midnight and waited outside, because the door sticks.

We went to hers. It was good and it was nothing like Nipigon, and neither of us said so, which I took as a kindness on both sides. She was moving to Rotterdam in the spring for a job in logistics. She said the word logistics the way you say a diagnosis you have made peace with.

At three my phone was at six percent. I lay in a stranger's bed in a city I had lied about, warm, and lost.

## 22. Ghent, then a bus, January

By now everybody knows that the cheapest way across Europe in winter is the night bus, and everybody is right about why.

Twenty-six hours. Amsterdam, Ghent, Cologne, Hamburg. A toilet locked at Bremen that stayed locked. A Bulgarian driver who took corners like a man being timed.

In Ghent I stayed three nights above a bicycle repair with a woman Roos knew. I looked at a painting in a cathedral for as long as they would let me. I came out onto a wet square feeling nothing I could use.

The man in 14C was Slovak, a welder going to a job in Esbjerg. We talked from midnight to Hamburg about his daughter's asthma. He was handsome and did not seem to know. Nothing charged either way. At Hamburg he gave me half a bar of chocolate and shook my hand like a colleague.

I had a job in Bergen I had lied to get. The lie was two years of front of house in Montreal. It held until June, which is longer than most of my arrangements.

## 23. Bergen, February

Rain on a skylight still puts me in Bergen.

It rained twenty-two days of my first month. The Norwegians were pleased about this in a manner I did not immediately understand, which is that the rain is theirs. The weather is a thing they own and you do not.

Blaž Rozman came from Maribor. Fourth year of a doctorate at the Centre for Digital Narrative, a real institute on a hill with good coffee and a view of a container port.

His subject was the sexual orientation of characters who do not exist.

He had a corpus of nine thousand non-player characters, avatars and AI influencers. He was coding each of them for what it was permitted to want. Most were permitted nothing. A number were permitted a person of the opposite sex, briefly, in a cutscene. Twenty-six had been given a same-sex partner and then patched.

He was not in charge of his own dataset. A licence sat with a company in Malmö. He had to ask them, in writing, before he could publish a table.

I asked him whether it depressed him. He said no, it clarified him, which is a Slovenian answer.

Bad posture. Beautiful hands. A laugh he did not use on strangers. He cooked one dish well and everything else at a level I would call brave. He asked me questions and remembered the answers a week later. Nobody had done that for me in a long time.

I fell in love with him at a bus stop on Nygårdsgaten while he explained metadata licensing. I would like to report that the moment had dignity.

## 24. Bergen, March

You are supposed to go twice a year.

I had not been since Montreal. A molar on the lower left had begun to speak to me at night. In Norway an adult pays for her own mouth.

Terje's practice was above a shoe shop. The receptionist quoted 1,650 kroner for the examination in a tone that suggested she had watched people leave.

Fifty-one, married, two daughters at the Katedralskole. A wedding ring on the tray beside the instruments, because of the gloves.

Here is what a dentist has. He has your whole face for forty minutes. He has a lamp and a reason. His hands are already in your mouth and he talks the entire time in a low voice about nothing, about the ferry, about your accent, and you cannot answer. Being unable to answer turned out to be a condition I should have known more about myself.

At the second appointment he asked where I was from. I said Montreal. He said, ah, and did not follow it up, and I decided on the spot.

It took a fortnight. His office on a Thursday afternoon, which he did not appear to have arranged and had arranged.

I was not troubled about the wife. I thought about her twice, both times in the stairwell.

That month I was in love with Blaž, going to Terje on Thursdays, working five dinner services a week. The arithmetic did not distress me. What distressed me was a new kind of tiredness. Not sleep. I was available to everybody, all day, in two languages, at a high standard, and the standard was slipping in small places. A name forgotten. A message read and not answered for a week.

Blaž said, you have gone quiet.

I said I was tired.

He said, from what, kindly. I could not answer that either.

## 25. Florø, April

A year before, almost to the week, I had stood in a chain pharmacy in southern Alberta while a stranger rang through a fifty-two dollar box. I had thought that was as much as a Sunday could hold.

I took the express boat north on the Friday. Five hours up the coast, the islands going by low and bare and the colour of a wet dog, oil platforms out west somewhere in the murk. Sixty-one degrees. The boat smelled of diesel and instant coffee.

Florø is a small town that services oil. I went because Blaž was at a conference in Tampere and because I wanted to be somewhere nobody could reach inside a day.

The Bunnpris was on the way back from the harbour. I went in for bread and a bag of the salty liquorice I had learned to eat.

A woman went down in the aisle with the tinned fish.

She was about sixty, in a red anorak, and she went down without putting a hand out, which is the part I keep. Her basket came apart on the floor. Then her back arched and her heels went at the linoleum and a sound came out of her that had nothing to do with a person. It went on. It went on much longer than I would have believed. Her handbag was still on her arm.

Two men took charge. One went to his knees, got a folded coat under her head, turned her. The other stood and shouted for the manager in a level voice.

A girl in a Bunnpris polo came at a run with a phone already at her ear.

The rest of us stood at the ends of the aisle.

There was nothing to do; others were doing it. I did not step forward. I did not look away. I noticed that her shoes were good.

The ambulance took nine minutes. When they lifted her she had come back partway and was saying a word I did not have.

I bought the bread. I did not buy the liquorice. I have not been able to explain the liquorice to myself since.

## 26. Bergen, April

Everyone says the shock is at the time. The shock was at the time and then it was for a month afterwards.

I got back on the Sunday and did not sleep properly again in that country. Not nightmares. I would be prepping mise en place at four in the afternoon and my hands would be elsewhere, and I would be standing in the aisle with the tinned fish, and a woman I had never met would go down again without putting a hand out.

Everything carried a charge. Blaž's hand on the back of my neck. Terje's stairwell. The clarinet in Amsterdam at seven in the morning. A man laughing on the Fløibanen. All of it came in at one volume, which is not a way you can live.

On the Wednesday Blaž made the one dish and asked me twice what had happened up there. I told him accurately. He was good about it and it made no difference.

On the Thursday I did not go to Terje. I did not message him either, which was the only cheap act of that spring.

On the Friday I booked Schiphol for the Sunday. Norwegian, 06:55. The second bag was 590 kroner, which I paid without the usual argument with myself.

I told Roos I was coming back. She said the Portuguese boy had returned and left again.

Blaž drove me to Flesland at half past four in the morning. He got out of the car, which he did not have to do, and held onto me in the drop-off lane for longer than the sign permits. He said, this is not the end of it. I said no. Both of us were guessing.

The plane went up through the cloud and came out on top into a light I had not seen in three months. I put my head against the window. I did not sleep this time. Somewhere over the North Sea I made the first honest decision of the year, which was to eat the breakfast.
