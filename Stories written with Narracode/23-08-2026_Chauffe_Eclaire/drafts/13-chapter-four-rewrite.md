## 21. Amsterdam, November

I had told my mother I had a friend in Amsterdam. Then I had one, which is how most of my lies have gone.

Wim printed art books on the ground floor and the whole house smelled of ink and damp paper. He would not take money for the back room. This worried me until I understood that he wanted an audience. He played clarinet at seven in the morning, badly, and apologised at eight, and by December I could hear from the apology how the printing had gone.

Roos had a kitchen on the Jan Evertsenstraat and took me on because a Portuguese boy had gone home and his apron was still on the hook. I wore it. By the end of service my clogs had a slick on them and my forearms smelled of dill and my hands were somebody else's hands, wet and red to the wrist.

Sanne found me. I had been arranging it in my head since the picnic shelter, and then Anouk put up a photograph of the Jetta going back to the rental and I recognised the ramp.

She came at closing and stood under the awning with her hood up and did not come in, because the door sticks and she knew it, which meant she had been by before and not come in.

Her room was over a bakery and too warm. It was good and it was nothing like the rain outside Nipigon, and neither of us said so, which I took as a kindness on both sides. She was going to Rotterdam in the spring for a job in logistics. She said the word logistics the way you say a diagnosis you have made your peace with.

I woke in the dark with the bakery starting up underneath us and the smell of it coming through the floor, in a city I had lied about, in a bed I liked, already working out how to leave.

## 22. Ghent, then a bus, January

By now everybody knows the cheapest way across Europe in winter is the night bus, and everybody is right about why.

The window sweats and you sleep against your own coat sleeve and wake with the seam printed down your cheek. A toilet locked outside Bremen and never opened again. A driver who took the corners like a man being timed.

In Ghent I slept over a bicycle repair and heard him truing wheels before it was light, that small ringing, over and over, before anything else in the street was up. I went and looked at a painting in a cathedral until the attendant started to move around behind me. A lamb. Blood going into a cup. A crowd of people who all already knew. I came out onto a wet square with nothing in my hands.

The man across the aisle was Slovak, a welder going to a job in Esbjerg, and he talked from midnight to Hamburg about his daughter's asthma, holding his hands apart to show me the size of the machine that helps her. He was handsome and did not seem to know it. Nothing charged either way. At Hamburg he gave me half a bar of chocolate and shook my hand like a colleague.

I had a job in Bergen I had lied to get. The lie was front of house in Montreal. It held longer than most of my arrangements.

## 23. Bergen, February

Rain on a skylight still puts me in Bergen.

It rained the way it does there, sideways off the harbour, then straight down, then sideways again. Nobody carries an umbrella. An umbrella is an argument you lose. Everybody's wool gave off the same smell on the tram and I stopped noticing it, which is how you know you live somewhere.

The dark came in the middle of the afternoon and had a colour to it, a blue you could still read by, and then it went.

Blaž Rozman came from Maribor and had a desk at the Centre for Digital Narrative, an institute up a hill with good coffee and a view of the container port.

His subject was the sexual orientation of characters who do not exist.

On his screen a woman in a tavern said the same three lines to me while we talked. She could be given a husband. She could not be given a wife; the option was in the menu and returned an error, and he kept a folder of the errors. He was coding each of them for what it was permitted to want. Most were permitted nothing at all. A few were permitted a person of the opposite sex, briefly, in a cutscene, before the game moved along. Some had been given a same-sex partner and then patched.

He kept the patch notes. He read them out to me once in bed, in the voice people use for horoscopes.

He was not in charge of his own data. A licence sat with a company in Malmö and he wrote to them in English to ask whether he might publish a table, and they took their time saying no.

Bad posture. Hands I looked at. A laugh he kept for people he knew. He cooked one dish well and everything else at a level I would call brave. He asked me questions and remembered the answers a week later, which nobody had done for me in a long time.

I fell in love with him at a bus stop on Nygårdsgaten while he explained a licensing problem, the rain going along the ground sideways, his hood up, his face wet. I would like to report that the moment had dignity.

## 24. Bergen, March

You are supposed to go twice a year.

I had not been since Montreal. A molar on the lower left had started speaking up at night, a thin wire of pain that came and went on its own schedule. In Norway an adult pays for her own mouth.

Terje's practice was over a shoe shop. The waiting room had a fig tree and a rack of magazines about cabins. The charge for an examination was more than I made in a night and the receptionist said it in the tone of a woman who has watched people leave.

He was fifty-one, married, two daughters at the Katedralskole. The ring came off onto the tray because of the gloves.

Here is what a dentist has. He has your whole face. He has a lamp, and a reason, and both hands in your mouth, and he talks the entire time in a low voice about the ferry, about your accent, about nothing at all, and you cannot answer.

The bib on its little chain with the alligator clip. The suction hooked over the lip, going dry against the inside of the cheek. The taste of the glove, which is not rubber exactly, but the powder on it. His forearm came across my collarbone when he reached for something, and stayed there, and neither of us mentioned it.

At the second appointment he asked where I was from. I said Montreal. He said, ah, and did not follow it up, and I decided then.

A Thursday afternoon in his office, which he did not appear to have arranged and had arranged.

I was not troubled about the wife. I thought about her twice, both times on the stairs, which smelled of shoe glue from the shop below.

That month I was in love with Blaž and going to Terje on Thursdays and the two things sat in me side by side and did not fight.

What was happening to me was a tiredness I had not had before, and it was not sleep. I was pleasant to everybody all day, in two languages, and I could feel the pleasantness going thin, the way a sauce goes when you have stretched it. I forgot a name at the pass. I read a message from my mother and did not answer until she sent another one asking if I was alive.

Blaž said, you have gone quiet.

I said I was tired.

He said, from what, kindly, and I could not answer that either.

## 25. Florø, April

A year before, almost to the week, I had stood in a chain pharmacy in southern Alberta while a stranger rang through a box of pills, and I had thought that was as much as a Sunday could hold.

I took the express boat north on the Friday. The islands went by low and bare, the colour of a wet dog. Platforms somewhere out west in the murk. The boat smelled of diesel and instant coffee and the seats were the blue they use for things that get wiped down.

Florø is a small town that services oil. I went because Blaž was at a conference in Tampere and because I wanted to be somewhere nobody could get to quickly.

The Bunnpris was on the way up from the harbour and I went in for bread and the salty liquorice I had learned to eat.

A woman went down in the aisle with the tinned fish.

She was about sixty, in a red anorak. She went down without putting a hand out, which is the part I keep. Her basket came apart on the floor. Then her back arched and her heels went at the linoleum and a sound came out of her that had nothing to do with a person, and it went on, and it went on much longer than I would have believed, and her handbag was still on her arm.

Two men took charge. One went to his knees, got a folded coat under her head, turned her. The other stood up and shouted for the manager in a level voice.

A girl in a Bunnpris polo came at a run with the phone already at her ear.

The rest of us stood at the ends of the aisle.

There was nothing to do; others were doing it. I did not step forward. I did not look away. I noticed that her shoes were good.

When they lifted her she had come back partway and was saying a word I did not have.

I bought the bread. I did not buy the liquorice. I have not been able to explain the liquorice to myself since.

## 26. Bergen, April

Everyone says the shock is at the time. The shock was at the time and then it went on.

I got back on the Sunday and did not sleep properly again in that country. Not nightmares. I would be prepping in the afternoon with the dark already arriving and my hands would keep working and I would be standing in the aisle with the tinned fish, and a woman I had never met would go down again without putting out a hand.

Somebody dropped a pan and I heard heels on linoleum.

I kept tasting the glove.

Everything carried a charge. Blaž's hand on the back of my neck. The stairs and their shoe glue. The clarinet through the floor in Amsterdam. A man laughing on the Fløibanen. It all came in at the same strength, and I began to flinch at the tram.

On the Wednesday Blaž made the one dish and asked me twice what had happened up there. I told him accurately. He was good about it and it made no difference.

On the Thursday I did not go to Terje. I did not message him either, which was the cheapest thing I did that spring.

On the Friday I booked a flight to Schiphol for the Sunday.

I told Roos I was coming back. She said the Portuguese boy had returned and left again.

Blaž drove me to Flesland in the dark and got out of the car, which he did not have to do, and held onto me in the drop-off lane past the point the sign allows. He said, this is not the end of it. I said no. Both of us were guessing.

The plane came up through the cloud into a light I had not seen since November. I put my face against the window. When the tray came I ate everything on it, including the butter.
