# Chauffé Éclairé

## 1. Parc-Extension, February

The listing said chauffé éclairé. On Ball Avenue I climbed the stairs with my phone between my teeth, lighting the steps, and found Sabrina trying to shut my door with a knife. She had changed out of her Jean Coutu shirt but still wore the badge. The knife was ours. She wanted it back when I was settled.

My room cost six hundred forty. I could stand beside the bed if I put my bag on it. Sabrina slept at the front in a mask that said DO NOT DISTURB, which she pushed up onto her forehead when she came out to tell us to shut the fuck up. Noon, usually. I liked her voice before she cleared it.

Milad came home with the delivery bags still strapped to him and ate over the sink while both apps called him back. He had a shiny burn on his forearm from the fryer at another job. Once I watched a drop of dishwater gather on the smooth part and he said what, and I said nothing, and he shook his arm over the plates.

Parc-Ex was finished, he said. Everybody knew.

I had just got here.

That is what finished means.

I went out again. There was a shop down the block with loose hair in the window, long black ropes hanging beside phone cases. A woman inside was eating an orange and dropping the peel into a receipt printer box. I wanted an orange so badly I walked back through the snow to buy some, then ate one on the stairs with my coat on. Downstairs the woman with the children had swept. I picked every seed out of the salt by my boots.

When the radiators stopped, the landlord sent his son. Kosta came with a tape measure and no gloves, rubbed his hands together, asked how I could stand the cold. I said I had called about that. He said the boiler was a whole conversation and began measuring the hallway.

He knelt to catch the metal hook under my baseboard. His shirt came up at the back. I had been alone all afternoon listening to Sabrina breathe through the wall and a truck outside reverse, stop, reverse again. I asked if he had to go somewhere.

He didn't.

Afterwards he lay beside me on the mattress and showed me a video of his dog refusing to cross a kitchen floor. Huge dog, tiny whine. We watched it again. Then he got up and reached for the tape.

I asked him not to write the measurement down. He rested the phone on his knee to type. I could still see the dog paused behind the little keyboard.

## 2. Côte-des-Neiges, May

At work I chose between answers written by a machine. One told a woman how to clean a stain out of a mattress. The other began by saying it understood how frustrating stains could be. I clicked the first. There were more stains, then a question about a dead hamster, then a man asking how to sound less angry in an email he had every reason to send angry. I took his side. We were the quality team.

The dentist from Algiers sat beside me, eating fennel from a plastic tub. He could do the work without moving his mouth. Mine kept forming the sentences and by lunch my jaw hurt. On the bus home I read the advertisements the same way. Teeth, training, a woman with her arms thrown wide over a loan.

Fadwa cooked when she came off shift. I heard the knife first, through my bedroom wall, then the pan, and I would lie there intending to sleep until the onions caught and I had to get up. She cooked in her bra with her work trousers unbuttoned. The kitchen window was level with the pavement. Shoes went past our heads.

She left me a plate under foil for the morning. Sometimes I ate it cold over the sink, sometimes I sat on the floor beside her while she finished cooking and we watched a man online repair a handbag neither of us would ever own. She hated the colour. We watched him finish.

Her boyfriend honked instead of coming to the door. The first Friday I went to the window and gave him the finger. He honked again. Fadwa laughed, then told me to get away from there, and I went out through the back with my jacket dragging on the steps.

I walked until the shops shut. At a laundromat a woman was folding enormous pink underpants on top of a dryer. She smoothed each pair with both palms. I sat watching somebody's washing turn. The woman asked if the machine had eaten my money. I said no and left.

In May Fadwa needed a letter saying she had lived in the basement since 2025. It had been 2027. I said yes while she was still explaining and typed it on my work laptop during the break. She stood behind me with a hand on my shoulder, correcting an accent. I couldn't find the key. Her hand moved to the back of my chair and I found it.

She read the letter twice. My French was getting better, she said.

I printed an extra copy in case somebody lost the first one.

## 3. Verdun, July

The woman in the cooling centre had brought frozen peas for the baby. She kept moving the bag from its neck to her own face, apologising whenever the baby made a noise. The baby liked the bag. It kicked when she took it away. Across from us an old man stood over a crossword balanced on the seat of a chair he wouldn't sit in.

I went outside to smoke and came back damp all the way through my shirt. The woman let me use the peas.

Sam had central air on Wellington. I met him at the dep while he was explaining orange wine to the cashier, who had already scanned it. Sam asked if I had tried any. I wanted something cold; I went with him.

His place had a Peloton facing the window and one fork. He ate with chopsticks while I used the fork, and when a noodle fell into my lap he reached to pick it up before either of us thought about that. His fingers were cold from the bottle. I stayed.

He worked in climate tech at a desk that rose when he pressed a button. In the mornings I lay in bed listening to him agree with people. Sometimes the desk went up during a disagreement. I could see his bare legs below his meeting shirt, the heels lifting as he argued. I told my own work the internet was down.

We spent the afternoons together with the curtains shut. Later, while he talked about carbon markets, I followed the bead of water down his glass until it reached his thumb. He wanted me to ask a question. I asked if he had anything else to eat.

There was cold rice. We fried it with the last egg and ate straight from the pan. He was better company when he had something to do with his hands.

His girlfriend was coming back Sunday. He told me while I was looking for a clean towel, opening cupboards I already knew. I slammed one and it bounced back into my face. For a moment neither of us said anything. Then I laughed so hard I had to sit on the bath.

I said I'd come for the air conditioning.

He knew. It had still been nice, hadn't it?

Yes. I took the wet towel off the hook and hung it over the shower rail so it would dry before she came.

## 4. Hochelaga-Maisonneuve, September

Somebody at the party gave me a shift unloading chairs. I wrote the address on a beer box and tore off the flap. Then somebody else asked whether I had ever worked a bar, which I had, and whether I owned black trousers, which depended on how dark the bar was. I went looking for another drink with work in my pocket.

The party was on Adam Street. A cousin had a guitar and knew the beginning of every song. On the balcony the women arguing about the union vote paused to help each other light cigarettes, then resumed where they had left off. I stayed out there until my drink was cold.

Vincent fixed motorcycles on Ontario. He had a grey beard and black along the sides of his nails, even after washing. When I said quality team he asked where. His ex-wife had done quality at Bombardier. I explained the answers on the screen, the helpful and the unhelpful, and he asked what happened if neither was any good.

You picked one.

He waited. I drank.

Upstairs at his place the solvent smell came through the floor. He moved a stack of folded clothes off the bed and was so careful with me I wanted to grab his hand and stop him being careful. I left my own hands under my head. A motorcycle went past outside; we both listened until it had gone.

Later he sat with his back to me, trying to turn his shirt the right way out. His son was at Sainte-Justine. Since June. Eight years old. He said aggressive and I waited for the rest of the sentence, but that was the word they had given him.

His shoulders moved. There was no sound. I put my hand out, got as far as the sheet behind him and began picking at a hard bead of laundry soap caught in the weave. He kept working at the sleeve.

What did the boy like?

Motorcycles, obviously.

He laughed through his nose and wiped it on the shirt. I asked which ones. He showed me on his phone, correcting the way I said Ducati. Downstairs someone was trying the shutters. He called out that he was coming and stayed where he was.

## 5. Saint-Henri, November

I was painting the kitchen when the landlord's letter came. There was paint on the envelope before I opened it. Major works, temporary vacancy, a paragraph about cooperation. They offered two thousand if I left. The tribunal date was so far away I read the year again.

I finished behind the stove. Nobody was going to see behind the stove. I had dragged it out and scraped off the grease and now I wanted that strip of wall white.

The Portuguese couple downstairs stopped answering their door. The other tenants took the money. I kept getting the previous tenant's mail, which was how I found her on Bourget, in a place where the kitchen table blocked half the refrigerator. Her cat had one ear and stood on my bag until I put it on a chair.

She asked my rent. Eleven ninety. She had paid six ten and said it again, looking at the cat, as though he had been there when we agreed on the first number and might help.

Her husband had died in the back room by the window. I said I was sorry.

Don't be, she said, it was the good room.

She wanted to know whether they had cut down the lilac. They hadn't. Whether the upstairs toilet still made that noise. It did. She laughed and leaned back and for a while we lived there together, complaining about the toilet, though her table was digging into my leg and I could hear someone in the next apartment crushing cans.

Renée, I told her when she asked my name.

Renée, listen. Don't take the two thousand.

I said I'd think about it. Outside, I missed a call offering an evening on coat check and called back before I'd put my gloves on. The job was gone. I bought a hot cheese pastry and burned the roof of my mouth walking to the bus. All the way home I worried the loose skin with my tongue.

I took the money. The stove went back against the wet paint and stuck.

## 6. Griffintown, January

Marie-Ève could strip a bed while talking on the phone and leave the fitted sheet folded over her arm. I kept catching mine under the mattress. She'd come back from the bathroom, still talking, lift the corner for me with her knee and go on with whatever her daughter had done. By the time I learned, she had stopped checking.

We cleaned short-term rentals. The booking app showed you the gaps. Sometimes a guest was still there when I let myself in, sitting in underwear eating delivery noodles, offended by the arrival of the person who would remove the smell of his noodles. I went down and waited beside the recycling. That waiting wasn't paid.

On Peel I worked around a woman filming herself unpacking. She took a dress out of tissue, looked delighted, put it back and did it again. I needed to vacuum where she was standing. She said just a second. In the lobby afterwards I caught my reflection holding the vacuum hose out in front of me and tried her delighted face.

I found a wedding ring and put it in the drawer. In another unit there was a gun in a bedside table. I shut the drawer with my hip and finished the bathroom. Marie-Ève said never look in the fridge. She meant if I had already finished for the day.

The Ottawa Street unit was often empty on Tuesdays. I started with a shower. The water hit the back of my neck hard enough to make me bend forward and I stayed until the mirror disappeared, then dried myself on a towel I would have to wash. I lay down while the load was running.

When I woke the windows were black. Across the courtyard a man was watering a plant in his underwear, lifting each leaf to get underneath. I watched him finish. There was nowhere I needed to be until morning.

After that I checked the bookings before I packed my cleaning bag. I left nothing in the unit, not even a toothbrush. Wipe the sink, pick a hair off the pillow, go back for the hair you only thought you picked up. I could lose the work. I kept sleeping there.

Chauffé. Éclairé. I ate toast in bed with the bedside lamp on and my bare feet outside the blanket. Crumbs stuck to the soles when I got up. I put another load in and went back to bed until it finished.

## 7. Mile End, March

My mother still had the apartment on Waverly. She stood at the good windows with a mug of tea she had forgotten to drink, watching the schoolyard. Her knees hurt on the stairs. Mrs. Kalogeras across the hall listened for the mail truck and opened her door before the driver had got out.

I had a bag of rolls from a catering shift. My mother cut one, pressed the middle with her thumb and said they froze these. Then she ate it over the sink. I took the rest out of the bag so they wouldn't sweat.

Was I seeing anyone?

Sort of.

Sort of was fine, she said. Sort of was most of it.

I was looking for the bread knife in the drawer where it had always been. My hand went right to the back, past the grater. She said she had moved it and reached across me, and I backed into the table hard enough to knock the tea over. We both grabbed for the mug.

Leave it, she said. Get the cloth.

The cloth was where it had always been.

She told me about Denis while I wiped the floor. In 1994 she had slept with him in the stairwell, on the landing between our place and the one above. He sold speakers. She couldn't remember whether he had given her a discount before or afterwards. Mrs. Kalogeras had come out for the mail and gone straight back in.

I sat on my heels with the cloth in my hand. My mother was laughing and trying to keep the roll in her mouth.

Those speakers?

Yes, those speakers. Did I want soup?

I did. While she heated it I moved the plant off one of them and put some music on, too loud for a school afternoon. She told me to turn it down from the kitchen. I turned it down a little.

## 8. Villeray, June

Ahmed repaired elevators. I met him at the taps on Villeray, filling a bottle I had found in my bag with something green dried around the mouth. He watched me rinse it and offered his. I said I was nearly done. He waited without watching, which made me hurry more.

The smoke had got into the apartment even with the windows shut. I wedged a wet towel under the balcony door and lay on the floor near it, breathing the wet cotton. The delivery app kept offering a bonus if I went out. I turned the phone over. Then I turned it back to look.

Ahmed came with a fan. It moved the same air faster, but I liked having him there to complain about it. He took the cover off and cleaned the blades in my sink while I held the motor away from the water. Grey fluff came off in strips. He had a pale band on his wrist where his watch usually sat.

He disliked the new buildings. He started explaining the doors, the sensors, a part nobody could get without replacing the whole assembly, and caught me looking at his wrist.

What?

I said show me the part. He pulled up a photograph. I looked at the photograph.

He stayed that night and came back another. In the morning he put his head under the tap to wet his hair, then used the reflection in the toaster to flatten it. I wanted to go downstairs with him. I had a shift later sorting returned clothes in a warehouse, checking the armpits before the tags went back on. I let him go and sat on the edge of the bed until I was late.

On Sunday the air cleared. Everybody came out onto the balconies with whatever they were doing. A woman shelled peas into a bowl. The kids on Casgrain screamed and I could hear a fork beating eggs in the apartment below. I went out without a jacket and walked past the bus stop, past the shop where I meant to buy milk, down to the tracks where the weeds were hot and smelled green again.

I sat on the concrete barrier until my thighs hurt. Trains were shunting somewhere out of sight. I wanted one to come past. None did.

Back home, Ahmed had sent a picture of the elevator he was inside, his boots on the roof of the car. I enlarged it to see where he was standing. Then I opened the listings. Rosemont, near the tracks. Chauffé, éclairé. There was a photograph of a kitchen with no stove and a chair facing the wall.

I wrote to ask when I could see it. Then I went out for the milk.
