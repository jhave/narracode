## 15. Squamish, April

Kieran said Squamish the way other people say a person's name, and in April I took the bus up the Sea to Sky to find out what he meant by it. Four months of deferral. I had thought about his wrists in three provinces.

He lived with five climbers in a house on Third with chalk in the carpet and a rack in the hall worth more than everything I have owned in my life. He was browner and thinner. He had a duvet with no cover on it, and I have thought about that since, because a man sleeping under a bare duvet has made a decision and is at peace with it.

I wanted him for four months. Then I had him and it was good, it was really good, and by Sunday afternoon I was on my back doing arithmetic about buses.

He was generous and unhurried and completely serious about it, the way he was about rock. He asked what I wanted and then did that, which men describe as a technique and is actually just paying attention. Twice he laughed in the middle. I liked him better for the laughing than for any of the rest of it.

I had carried the thing nine hundred kilometres. He had only been living here.

Monday he had a route. He said come Thursday, and meant it. I said maybe.

I didn't go Thursday. On the Wednesday I got on a bus going east with a coffee I couldn't drink because of the switchbacks, and somewhere past Lillooet a woman across the aisle told me her whole divorce, and I listened to all of it, and it was better than Thursday would have been.

## 16. The Okanagan, June

Cherries first, then the packing house when my hands gave out. Piece rate on the ladders, hourly inside, and inside is worse money and better for you, which everybody tells you and nobody believes until their thumbs stop closing.

I covered Manon's shift the second week and she covered mine for four days in July, and that is how you make a friend in a valley in summer.

Manon was from Trois-Rivières, had done six seasons, drove a Corolla with a mattress in it and a milk crate of paperbacks; she called everybody *chose*. Deke ran the line and was gentle to the pickers and vile to the drivers. A couple from Chile, Andrés and Sol, who had a real romance going on in the bunkhouse and did not care who heard it, and good for them, honestly, because the rest of us were listening.

It got to thirty-eight and stayed. In the bunkhouse at night with the fans going you lay on top of everything in your underwear and did not touch anybody because touching was worse.

Then you touched somebody anyway.

Deke, once, badly, in the walk-in, and we both laughed about it afterward and never referred to it again. Sol's cousin Tomás who came for eleven days to help with the crush and had a laugh like a door and forearms out of a painting, and eleven days is a real length of time when it is thirty-eight degrees and nobody is anybody's anything.

Manon said, *chose*, you're going to burn yourself out.

I said probably.

She said, no, I mean actually, put a hat on.

The smoke came in on the twentieth from something past Merritt and the valley went under cover for nine days, the light like the inside of a jar, the cherries still coming, everybody working with a mask pushed down under the chin because you cannot pull a ladder in a mask. Precy's line came back to me in the smoke. It never used to be June either.

## 17. Southern Alberta, July

His name was Boyd and he had a good truck and a bad house and I liked him for about thirty hours.

The condom came off inside me. This happens; it had not happened to me since I was twenty-three; and it happened on a Saturday, which is the detail that mattered.

Sunday morning in a town of eleven hundred. Boyd was decent about it and drove. Understand that decent is the whole review: he got up, he drove, he did not make a speech.

Pharmacy closed. Drive forty minutes. That one closed too, a paper sign, *back Monday*. Drive to the next one. Sit in the truck outside the Shoppers in a town with a Canadian Tire while it turns eleven. Go in. Walk past the shampoo twice because there is a girl on till who looks about nineteen. Go to the counter anyway. Say it out loud with a man behind you at the blood-pressure machine. Watch the pharmacist be completely normal about it, thank God, and ask for your card.

Out of province.

No coverage. Fifty-two dollars.

I had fifty-two dollars. That is the only reason this is a story about a Sunday and not a story about something else, and I know exactly how thin that is, and so does every woman I have ever worked a season with.

Read the box in the parking lot. Sooner is better, it says. Swallow it dry in the truck because the water is in the back under the tarp. Wait. Feel nothing. Feel, an hour later, extremely something.

Boyd bought me a coffee and a bag of ketchup chips and put the radio on low without being asked, which I am still, a year on, prepared to call gallantry.

By Tuesday I was in Medicine Hat and by Thursday I was gone.

## 18. Winnipeg, August

Emerson picked up on the fourth ring, which is three more than Kat.

The bakery had let her go in March and she was doing mornings at a place on Corydon and living with a man named Griff, and none of that was in the messages about the dog. She said come anyway. I went anyway.

Griff was fine. Griff was so fine. He made a curry and asked good questions about the Okanagan and did not once look at me the way I had gone eleven hundred kilometres to be looked at.

Emerson had cut her hair. Her hands were the same, flour or no flour, and I watched them on the wooden spoon and on Griff's shoulder and on the back of a chair, and I sat in that kitchen with my face doing the pleasant thing while I understood that I had built nine days into a country.

We had one hour on the back steps after Griff went up.

She said, that week was a good week.

I said, it was nine days.

She said, was it, and I could not tell if she was being kind or if she genuinely did not know, and I have decided not to decide.

I slept on the couch. In the morning she made me eggs and put too much salt on them the way she does, and I got the two o'clock bus, and she hugged me at the door with the heat coming in this time.

## 19. North of Superior, September

A man in a truck for eleven hours is either the worst idea you have had or the whole point of being alive and you find out somewhere around Marathon.

Ted hauled for a company out of Thunder Bay, had a granddaughter's drawing taped over the tach, drove with two fingers, talked about his knees. Not that. Nothing like that. He bought me a sandwich in Wawa and told me his wife had been dead six years and that the trick was to keep the truck moving, and dropped me at a gas station at nine at night with the last light going pink off the water, and said, careful with yourself, and I have thought about the way he said *with*.

That gas station is where I met Anouk and Sanne, Dutch, twenty-four and twenty-five, driving a rented Jetta from Halifax to Vancouver in three weeks because that is a thing you can believe is possible when you are twenty-four.

Six hundred kilometres in a car with two Dutch girls, all the windows down, the north shore going by in the dark, Anouk driving with her knee while she rolled something, Sanne screaming the words to a song from before any of us were born, the lake on our right the whole night doing nothing and being enormous.

We took cover in a picnic shelter outside Nipigon when it came down at two in the morning. We got extremely wet and then we got extremely warm and I am not going to lay it out any further than that except to say that it was one of the best nights of my adult life and that in the morning we ate gas-station eggs and they went west and I went east and none of us pretended otherwise.

Sanne wrote her number on my arm. It came off in the rain outside Sault Ste. Marie.

I let it.

## 20. Toronto, October

Kat opened the door and said, you look terrible, and I have never been so pleased.

Pris had moved in properly. There was a plant with a name. I got the couch and eleven days and a series of increasingly frank conversations at the kitchen table about what I was doing, which Kat conducted like a person defusing something and Pris conducted like a person who thought I was a good time.

Dez came by once. It was fine. We are the sort of thing that is fine now.

I did shifts at a place on Ossington with a cover charge and a band that played covers, and a bartender named June who was not interested and made that clear in a way I found restful, and I made eleven hundred dollars in eleven days and did not spend it.

My mother said, Amsterdam, on the phone, in a tone with four things in it.

I said I have a friend there.

I do not have a friend there. I have a Dutch girl's number that came off my arm outside Sault Ste. Marie, and a job lead from Manon's cousin, and eleven hundred dollars, and a bag that has been packed since February.

Kat drove me to Pearson at five in the morning and did not get out of the car, because she does not do that, and said, call me from there, and I said I would, and I did.

Gate B34. Two hours early because I am my mother's daughter.

Boarding, an old man in the row ahead asked the attendant, in French, whether it would be cold, and she said, it is Amsterdam, sir, it is always a little cold, and he said, good.

The cabin got dark over Labrador. I put my head against the window and thought about Kieran's hands and Sanne's mouth and Boyd's radio at low volume, one after another, without ranking them, and slept over the whole ocean.
