method — the voice engine

The brief asked for an engine, not an impression. The register was therefore specified as measurable properties rather than as an adjective, and a validator (tools/tillman_check.py) was written to enforce them between the compositional and revision passes. Draft 1 failed. Draft 3 passes.

Targets: median sentence ≤ 7 words · ≥ 65% of sentences ≤ 8 words · ≤ 8% over 14 words · any sentence over 12 words must break on a semicolon, with ≤ 10 words after it · figuration ≤ 2 per 1,000 words · paragraphs ≤ 6 sentences.

Final measurement: 1,430 words · 228 sentences · 38 paragraphs · mean 6.3 · median 6 · longest sentence 14 words · 75.9% at or under eight words · 2 figures in the whole story · zero em-dashes · zero hits against the house registry of machine tells.

On the source. Weird Fucks is the prosodic reference and nothing more. No sentence, character, scene or incident of Lynne Tillman’s appears here. What was extracted is structural — length distribution, figuration rate, the semicolon hinge, episodic sectioning, flatness at the turn — and rebuilt from first principles in this project’s reference/voice-engine.md. The validator contains no source text either, only thresholds.

What the instrument cannot see. It confirms varied sentence length; it cannot hear that the dominant sentence shape stays subject-verb-object. It counts figures; it cannot tell whether a located abstraction is placed somewhere brave or somewhere safe. The project’s own critique (critiques/critique-all-acts.md) records both failures against the brief, unfixed, for the human editing pass.

Chapter One

1. Parc-Extension, February

The listing said chauffé éclairé. It was neither. Ball Avenue. Second floor. A door that didn't close. Six hundred forty for the room. Four of us. One bathroom.

Sabrina had the front. She worked nights at the Jean Coutu. Slept in a sleep mask at noon. Woke up furious, every time. Milad had the back. He drove for two apps. A burn on his forearm. Fryer, at a third job.

He said Parc-Ex was finished. Everybody knew. I said I had just got here. He said that is what finished means.

Downstairs was a woman with three kids and a husband on nights. She kept the stairwell swept. Nobody asked her to.

The radiators went cold on a Tuesday. I called the landlord. The number rang somewhere in Laval. His son came instead. Kosta, twenty-four, tape measure and a phone. He measured the hallway. He measured my room with the door open. I asked about the heat. He said the boiler was a whole conversation.

We fucked on the floor because my bed was on the floor. He kept his socks on. The socks were fine. Afterwards he stood up and finished measuring. I asked him not to write the number down. He wrote the number down.

2. Côte-des-Neiges, May

Basement on Van Horne. Five ninety. No window in the room. Fifty minutes to work. Two buses. One bus and the metro when the bus didn't come.

The work was rating answers. A model wrote them, I said helpful or not helpful. Nineteen fifty an hour. They called us the quality team. On my row, a retired teacher. A kid from Laval. A man who had been a dentist in Algiers. The dentist was the fastest. He never said anything about it.

Fadwa had the other room. She was doing her papers. She cooked at two in the morning. Left me a plate with foil on it. I ate it cold at six. It was always better cold.

Her boyfriend came Fridays. He parked badly and honked instead of buzzing. Hate in the teeth, no argument in it. Some Fridays I sat on the steps until they were done.

In May she asked me for a letter. Saying she had lived there since 2025. She had lived there since 2027. I said yes before she finished the sentence. I typed it on my work laptop, on my break, badly. She read it twice and said my French was getting better. That was the nicest thing anyone said to me that year.

3. Verdun, July

Forty-one with the humidex. Six days. The city opened the cooling centres. Ours was a church basement on Galt. An old man did crosswords. He would not take the last chair.

A woman came in with a baby. And a bag of frozen peas. She put the peas on the back of its neck. The baby was fine. She was not.

Sam had central air on Wellington. He worked in climate tech. I met him in line at a dep that sold natural wine. He said the wine was orange. I said okay. His condo had a Peloton and one fork.

I stayed nine days. I told work my internet was down. We fucked in the afternoons because the afternoons were unbearable. He talked afterwards. Carbon markets, mostly.

On the ninth day he said his girlfriend was back Sunday. I said I was there for the air conditioning. He said he knew that. Then he said it was still nice, wasn't it. He looked at me. I said yes. It had been nice. Both of those were true and I have not worked out how.

4. Hochelaga-Maisonneuve, September

A party on Adam Street for someone I didn't know. Somebody's cousin played guitar badly and everyone let him. Two women on the balcony argued about a union vote. An hour of it.

Vincent was fifty-one and fixed motorcycles on Ontario. Grey in the beard, hands you noticed, a laugh that came late. He asked what I did. I said quality team. His ex-wife did quality at Bombardier. Before they cut the line.

We went to his place above the shop. It smelled of solvent. He was gentle. I had not expected it and did not want it.

Afterwards he sat on the edge of the bed. Back to me. He said his son had been at Sainte-Justine since June. The boy was eight. The word they used was aggressive. He cried without any noise, which is a skill.

I did not touch his back. I thought about touching his back. Pity sat in the wrists, useless. I asked what the boy liked. He said motorcycles, obviously, and then he laughed for real.

5. Saint-Henri, November

The renoviction letter came in a real envelope. That should have warned me. Major works. Six months. Two thousand to leave quietly. The tribunal gave me a date fourteen months out; I painted the kitchen anyway.

Four units in the building. Three took the money in a week. The Portuguese couple on one stayed and stopped answering the door.

The woman who had the place before me lived on Bourget now. I found her because the mail kept coming. I brought it over. She was seventy and had a cat with one ear.

She asked me what I was paying. I said eleven ninety. She said six ten. Twelve years. She did not have to think about it. She said her husband died in the back room, by the window. I said I was sorry. She said don't be, it was the good room.

Then she asked my name and I said Renée. She said Renée, listen. Don't take the two thousand. I took the two thousand.

6. Griffintown, January

I cleaned units for cash. Ninety a unit, four a day when there were four. Glass buildings. Wind off the canal. No grocery store, so everyone ordered. The keys were in lockboxes. The codes were all somebody's birthday.

I learned what people leave. Chargers, mostly. Contact lenses. Once a wedding ring. Once a gun. I put the ring in the drawer. Said nothing about the gun. Marie-Ève did the towers on Peel and had done it nine years. She said never look in the fridge; I looked in the fridge.

A unit on Ottawa Street got booked twice a month, maybe. I started sleeping there Tuesdays. I changed the sheets after. I told myself it was one night. Then it was the winter.

A stranger's bed on the eleventh floor. Heat at twenty-three. Chauffé. Éclairé. Comfort behind the ribs, stolen. I have never in my life been that warm. I want to say I was ashamed. I was not ashamed.

7. Mile End, March

My mother has the same apartment on Waverly since 1994. Three hundred then. Nine forty now, because he never renovated and never died. She has the good windows and the bad knees. Mrs. Kalogeras across the hall watches for the mail truck. It gives her the day.

My mother made tea and did not drink it. Her way. She asked if I was seeing anyone. I said sort of. She said sort of is fine. Sort of is most of it. We looked out at the schoolyard for a while.

She said she fucked a man in that stairwell in 1994. His name was Denis and he sold speakers. She said it like weather. Then she asked if I wanted soup. I said yes to the soup.

I sat in her kitchen and understood nothing new about her. I just liked her more, sitting there, in the good windows.

8. Villeray, June

Sixteen fifty-five, third floor. A balcony the size of a door. The smoke came the second week of June. It stayed eleven days. Orange light in the afternoon. Stay in, close everything. I closed everything. It came in anyway. Everything tasted of campfire.

The dep on Villeray taped a sign up: masks, four dollars. I met a man at the taps who fixed elevators. His name was Ahmed and he had opinions about buildings. The ones from the sixties were honest. The new ones were not. He stayed over twice. He left his socks the second time, on purpose I think. I have not decided about him.

On the Sunday the air cleared and everybody came outside at once. The whole street on the balconies, as if something had been announced. Nothing had been announced. The air was just clean. The kids were screaming on Casgrain and nobody told them to stop.

That night I opened the listings. Habit, not need. Chauffé, éclairé. Nine hundred. Rosemont, near the tracks. I wrote to ask if it was still available.

9. Toronto, August

Kat and I had been friends eleven years, first in Montreal and then not at all for four of them. Then again, because she picked up on the second ring like nothing had happened. She asked if I still smoked.

The Rosemont place went to somebody with a guarantor. I put what I had into two bags and gave the rest to the woman downstairs. The 6:40 takes seven hours in 2028. They have been fixing the corridor since I was in school.

Kat built sets for a company that made commercials for insurance companies; she had a wrecked shoulder from it, a girlfriend named Pris who was in Chicago more than she wasn't, a place on Sorauren she could not have got in any other year; she was rude to me inside an hour. I hadn't been talked to like that since March. I slept fourteen hours.

In Toronto nobody asks what you pay. They ask how long you've had it, which is the same question with the shame moved.

Dez came over on the Thursday to fix Kat's speakers and stayed for dinner. He did sound for other people's films and lived above a garage on Sterling; had toured with a band people my age pretend to remember, had a kid in Halifax he saw twice a year; he was easy. He put his arm along the back of the chair. I looked at his forearm the whole time Kat was telling a story about a producer. When she got to the part where the producer cries I laughed a beat late. Dez noticed that I had laughed late.

Nothing happened. He washed four plates, badly, and left at eleven. I stood in the kitchen with the water still running.

Ahmed texted from Montreal at one in the morning. Two lines about a building on Papineau and then, are you coming back. I typed and deleted. I said the elevators in Ontario are worse and he sent a laughing face. That was the last one for a while.

Kat came out for water in a t-shirt with a dead festival on it. She looked at me standing there.

She said, you're not visiting.

I said no.

She said okay. She drank the water and went back to bed. I have never in my life been so grateful to be seen and not asked about.

10. Northern Ontario, September

The train leaves Toronto at nine at night and reaches Winnipeg two days later. If it reaches Winnipeg. The freight owns the track, so passenger sits in a siding while eleven thousand feet of grain goes by in the dark. We sat four hours outside Hornepayne. Nobody explained it. Nobody in the car expected an explanation, which told me most of them had done this before.

Loreen had the seat across the aisle. She was going to a funeral in Sioux Lookout for a brother she hadn't spoken to since 2019. Twenty-two years with the school board; a thermos of rye and 7Up she was not hiding; she was funny about him. She said he was a bastard and then said it again more warmly.

Coach means you sleep sitting up with your coat as a pillow. You wake at four with your hip gone dead. The window black and your own face in it. The bathroom at the end of the car smelled like a public pool. I brushed my teeth over a steel sink with the floor moving under me and thought about nothing at all. That almost never happens.

Then the light came up over muskeg and rock and black spruce standing in water, thousands of them, and it went on being that for six hours. No town. A hydro cut every so often with the towers walking away north. Water the colour of tea.

I used to take pictures. I stood in the vestibule with the glass down and the noise enormous. I did not take a picture.

Loreen ate a sandwich at nine in the morning and offered me half without looking over. In the middle of it she said her brother had a way of standing in a doorway that made you feel the room was his. She said that when the call came her whole chest went loose, like a belt off.

I said I was sorry.

She said, don't be sorry yet, I haven't decided.

11. Winnipeg, September

Roy is Kat's cousin. He had a house on Lipton with three people in it, and a fourth who had left a bike. He worked nights loading for a courier, was kind in a bland unswerving way, made a pot of chili on the Sunday that lasted the week; he wanted nothing from me. I could feel my body scanning him for it anyway. Finding nothing. Going quiet, disappointed, then relieved.

It went to minus four on the second night. In September. Everyone in that house talked about it the way you talk about a diagnosis in the family, informed and bored.

In Winnipeg nobody finishes a conversation outside after the middle of October. They finish it in the doorway with the cold coming in. Somebody says okay, okay, and the door shuts on the rest of it.

Emerson had the room off the kitchen and worked at a bakery; up at three, home at noon, asleep by eight; we overlapped four hours a day. One bathroom. She had flour in the creases of her knuckles that did not come out. Her forearms were better than her face and she knew it. She wore short sleeves in a cold house.

We were in the kitchen on the Wednesday. She was cutting something and I was leaning on the counter beside her with my arms folded. Neither of us moved for the length of a song on the radio. The distance was maybe eight inches. I have thought about that eight inches more than about anything that happened afterward.

What happened afterward was quick and mostly good and did not repeat. On the Friday she went to her mother's in Selkirk and texted about a dog. I left on the Sunday. She hugged me at the door with the cold coming in, said travel safe, and shut it on the rest of it. I walked to the bus with my whole chest ringing like a struck pipe. Nine days. I had known her nine days.

12. Saskatchewan, late September

Warrick was driving a pickup and an empty stock trailer from Brandon to Strathmore. He took me because his wife makes him take people, he said, so he doesn't fall asleep. He was fifty-something, had been in the Forces, had a bad knee and a good radio; he talked for forty minutes, then didn't.

The land does not open. That is the wrong word and everybody uses it. It is already open. It stays open, and you go along inside it for nine hours. The only thing that changes is which elevator is on the horizon, and then which one, and then which one. By the middle of the afternoon your sense of your own size has quietly adjusted. It does not adjust back for a couple of days.

Canola gone over. Stubble. Sloughs with birds on them. A dead deer with its legs at angles you don't see in bodies that are all right.

Two combines working in a field the size of a district, dust standing up gold behind them. A grid road going straight for so long it stopped being a road and became a fact about the planet.

I asked Warrick for his phone at one point, to take a picture, and then handed it back without taking one. He did not ask why.

He dropped me at a gas station in Swift Current and shook my hand and said good luck out there. I stood with my bags at a pump under those lights that make everybody look ill. Nothing had happened. Nothing was going to.

I want to be honest that it was one of the better days.

13. Banff, October

They put me in the kitchen at a hotel with a French name and a Chinese owner. Staff accommodation was up the hill, four to a room, four hundred a month taken off the cheque before we saw it. That is where I got a cast for the first time since the Jean Coutu.

Tarn was from Newcastle, New South Wales, and had done two seasons; lifts in winter, pot-wash in the shoulder, could not be embarrassed by anything; his laugh carried to the loading bay. Precy had been there six years and ran the line without the title or the money for it. Small, fast, unimpressed; two kids in Cebu she called at five in the morning our time; she was the best of us. Kieran climbed on his days off and washed dishes on the others. He did not talk unless the subject was rock, at which point he could not be stopped.

We were funny at two in the morning in a way that does not survive being described. Friends. I had not had any in a while.

The fire started on the eleventh, on the BC side. By the thirteenth the whole valley was the colour of a nicotine ceiling and the mountains were a rumour with an edge on it. In October. Precy said it never used to be October and went back to plating.

Kieran and I had the bunks against the same wall with a curtain between that did not reach the floor. Nine nights. I knew his breathing before I knew his face properly. On the fifth night the alarm went at three for burnt toast on two. We all stood outside in the smoke in whatever we slept in, two hundred of us, coughing and laughing. He was beside me with his arms crossed and his hands pushed under them for the cold. I looked at his wrists. He caught me looking. Neither of us did anything about it for four more days.

Then he got a call about a route and a window and went to Squamish. That was that. It had been the best thing in my week for nine days running.

Tarn said, hard luck, and gave me half his cigarette.

Precy said, you'll be all right, and put me on garde manger. Which was a promotion. Neither of us mentioned it.

14. Vancouver, December

It rains here the way it rained the whole time I was there. Everyone tells you about it before you can say anything about it yourself.

I got a room in a house on Kingsway. Bernice had a parrot, and rules about the parrot. I did prep at a place on Fraser four days and cleaned an office tower two nights. In December it goes dark at four. The wet gets into the seams of your coat and stays there. You learn the smell of your own building.

Dez was in town for eight weeks doing post on a feature. He got my number from Kat. I let a day go by before answering, because I am not a child.

We had two good nights and then we had a bad one.

The bad one was nothing, which is what I want to put down. He said something about my mother that was a joke and was accurate. I laughed. Twenty minutes later I was crying in a Skytrain station at Broadway with my hood up and my hands shaking. It could have been the joke, or the accuracy, or the eight weeks, or the woman in Winnipeg with flour in her knuckles, or my father, who drove a truck, and stopped.

I want to be clear that I do not think it was my father.

Dez texted the next morning to see if I was okay and I said fine, and he said okay. That was the honest end of it. We saw each other twice more.

My mother called on the Sunday. She has the good windows and she wanted to tell me about a leak. Halfway through she stopped and said, are you eating. I said yes. She said, you're not. Then she told me about the leak for six more minutes.

The rain went sideways past the streetlight for the whole call.

Bernice knocked and said the parrot was doing the thing. I went and looked at the parrot doing the thing. It was funny.

Later, the fires again, but that is the spring.
