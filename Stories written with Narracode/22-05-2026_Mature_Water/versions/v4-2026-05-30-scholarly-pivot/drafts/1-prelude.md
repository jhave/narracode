Mid-21st century. A lecture hall in the kind of institute that prints its name in lower-case across the front of the building. Translation earbuds in maybe a third of the audience, glinting in the house light. On the back wall, twelve scripts run in soft live caption — Devanagari, Hangul, Mandarin, Arabic, an Igbo phrase he can read just well enough to know it is Igbo, and seven others he cannot read. On the podium, a small green light, steady. The model is following along. Beside the green light, a glass of water no one has touched.

Dayo Lim. The programme card calls him Research Communicator. He read this on the train, looked at it for a stop, looked out the window. His grandmother dried peppers on a tin roof at the edge of Ekiti forest and laughed at him when he tried to give names to what she was doing. His father wore the same suit-cut for twenty-eight years and once, in Seoul, across a kitchen table, said *aigu* — the sound a man makes setting down a weight he has decided not to mention. Three cities raised him. They did not reconcile in him; he stopped, some years ago, waiting for them to.

The room hushes. He walks to the podium without looking at anyone in particular. He sets a finger on the rim of the glass and does not lift it. He inhales once. On the back wall the captions begin to anticipate him — a faint trembling of script as the model adjusts its prediction window. He starts in the middle of a sentence. He had been thinking on the train; he keeps going from where the thinking was.
