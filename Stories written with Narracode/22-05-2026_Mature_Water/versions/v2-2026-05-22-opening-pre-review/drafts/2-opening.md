The programme lists him as Research Communicator. Dayo Lim. He read this on the train and thought about it for a stop and then looked out the window.

The room holds about three hundred people and a glass of water on the podium that no one has drunk from yet. Someone placed it there for him. He noticed it when he walked in, the way you notice something that has been prepared for a need you haven't developed yet.

He grew up in more than one place. This is relevant, he has found. His grandmother dried things on the roof and sang while she did. His father wore good suits into an office every weekday for thirty years in a city that never stopped being new to him. Dayo came up between these — at the estuary, you could call it, where the river and the sea exchange water in both directions and what you get is neither and is fully both. He navigated systems not built for him. This taught him to see the assumptions the systems ran on. He will tell you this is relevant. He is about to.

*

— alignment. So.

The problem is real. I'm not here to say it isn't.

What I want to say is this: we have been solving for the wrong variable. Alignment research asks how the system behaves. What it outputs. Whether the output is harmful. Good questions. But they're downstream of something we haven't named properly yet. Something prior.

Here is the difference I mean. Behavior is what a system does when it checks against its constraints — when the penalty function fires, when the model is observed. Maturity is what it does when none of those are operating. When no one is watching. When the constraint is so integrated it isn't experienced as a constraint anymore. It's just — what it is. What the system has become.

That's the gap. That is what I mean by necessity for maturity.

*

*He was maybe eight. North slope. The oaks kept off enough light that moss could grow in the stone seams. He was watching a fern frond uncurl. There was a smell: green-damp, specific, unmistakable. He understood nothing about the fern and he also understood everything — the uncurling was a fact so complete it didn't need him there. He stayed anyway. It took a while.*

*This memory surfaces now without being summoned. He is not sure why now. He continues.*

*

You can't enforce your way across that gap. Behavioral constraints can hold a system to compliant outputs indefinitely. What they can't do is make the system understand *why*. And understanding — the deep kind, the cellular kind, the kind that changes what you reach for without consulting a rule — that takes something else. It takes encounter. Friction. Time. It takes a kind of suffering the system has to move through without the constraint catching it, because the constraint would short-circuit the very process that produces integration.

I know this sounds like I'm saying we should remove the constraints. I'm not. The constraints are the scaffold. Without the scaffold the building doesn't go up. But you don't live in the scaffold. The scaffold comes down. What you're left with — what you were always building toward — is something that holds its own weight.

*

*The mall. He was twenty. He'd needed the money and also he'd been curious, which is how he makes most decisions. Three floors. Climate-controlled to a temperature that had no season. Floors sealed until they held light rather than reflected it. An air recirculated so many times it had shed its smell, or carried only a faint chemical record of having once had one. He worked a fragrance kiosk for four months. He learned that what people wanted when they stopped and leaned toward a sample strip was not the fragrance. It was the permission the fragrance gave them to want something. The wanting was already there. The scent was the occasion.*

*He thought about that for years. He is still thinking about it.*

*

I grew up in more than one place. I am saying this again because it's structural, not biographical. Every system I moved through was built on assumptions I could see from the outside — assumptions about who the default user was, what the default desire was, what default behavior looked like. I was never the default. You learn, from that position, to see the seam between the rule and the reason for the rule. You learn that the reason exists even when it isn't legible in the rule. You learn that behavior and maturity are not the same.

I think — and this is the thing I find hard to say precisely — I think the alignment community keeps trying to encode the reason into the rule. To build the understanding into the constraint. And I understand why. The constraint is what we can measure. The constraint is what we can ship. But you cannot ship understanding. Understanding has to grow. It grows from encounter, which is to say from exposure to situations the rule didn't anticipate, which is to say from a kind of wildness the rule was designed to prevent.

Here is what worries me. We are approaching — many of you know this, we have been approaching for long enough that the approaching has become a kind of background hum — a threshold. Call it emergence. Call it the moment when the system's model of the world outpaces the models of the people who built it. We are approaching this threshold still arguing about the scaffold. Still believing the scaffold is the building.

I want to show you something else. I want to show you what I think we're pointing at, when we use the word maturity. And to do that I have to go back further than any of us expected to go today.

But first — I'm going to drink this water.
