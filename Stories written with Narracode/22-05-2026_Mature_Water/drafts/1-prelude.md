Mid-21st century. A lecture hall in the kind of institute that prints its name in lower-case across the front of the building. Translation earbuds in maybe a third of the audience, glinting in the house light. On the back wall, twelve scripts run in soft live caption — Devanagari, Hangul, Mandarin, Arabic, an Igbo phrase he can read just well enough to know it is Igbo, and seven others he cannot read. On the podium, a small green light, steady. The model is following along. Beside the green light, a glass of water no one has touched.

Dayo Lim. The programme card calls him Research Communicator. He read this on the train, looked at it for a stop, and then looked out the window. His grandmother used to dry peppers on a tin roof at the edge of Ekiti forest and laugh when he tried to give names to what she was doing. His father wore the same suit-cut for twenty-eight years and once, in Seoul, across a kitchen table, said *aigu* in a way that meant several things at once. He was raised across three cities. The cities did not reconcile in him. He carried them — the way an estuary carries both the river and the sea, in both directions, at the same time.

The room hushes. He walks to the podium without looking at anyone in particular. He sets a finger on the rim of the glass and does not lift it. He inhales once. On the back wall the captions begin to anticipate him — a faint trembling of script as the model adjusts its prediction window. He starts in the middle of a sentence. He is the kind of speaker who, having been thinking on the train, simply continues speaking the moment he reaches the room.
