# POETICS

## Project

*The Author Was Already Dead.* A short literary fiction in the grammar of Raymond Chandler, transplanted into contemporary time. The triggering situation: an author is found dead. The story is not a whodunit. The death is the pressure under the sentences; what the prose investigates is a literary economy in 2026 — agents, archives, residency programs, prizes, the unread book, the curated obituary, the LLM-assisted estate.

At the center of the story is a love affair. The dead author had fallen in love with the word — not with language in the abstract, but with the particular experience of voice-transcribing into a mobile phone. The phone is the love interest. It occupies the structural slot a femme fatale occupies in classical noir, but it is not a femme fatale: it is a 6.1-inch glass rectangle that translated his voice into text and made him, in his last years, a different writer. It is also what killed him.

## Four-act structure

The piece is targeted at four acts. Drafts are written one scene at a time; the act-level frame is a tension surface, not an outline to be filled in.

- **Act I — Arrival.** The agent enters the apartment. The room is the room. Objects are introduced as potentials. *(Scene 1 = drafted as `1-arrival.md`.)*
- **Act II — The lover.** The phone is found, recovered, charged, opened. The agent encounters the corpus of voice-transcribed prose. She begins to understand the affair: that in his last decade Marek had stopped typing and begun walking-and-speaking, and that the writer who emerged from that practice was someone she had never represented.
- **Act III — The killing.** The story commits to a mechanism of death. The death happens *inside* the act of dictation — the transcription captures the moment Marek stops speaking. The reader experiences the death through the text the phone produced, not through a coroner's report. The phone is murder weapon and lover at once; the romance and the killing are the same gesture.
- **Act IV — Disposition.** The agent must decide what becomes of the corpus. Publish it as-is, with the algorithmic mishearings preserved as part of the work. Edit it into something legible. Delete it. Keep it for herself. Echoes Act II: she has spent the story falling in love with the voice the phone produced, and the disposition is her reckoning with that.

## Characters

- **Marek Halsey.** Sixty-seven. Novelist. Had one widely admired book at twenty-nine and was widely understood to be writing the next one for the next thirty-eight years. A meticulous sentence-arranger by training; in his last decade he stopped typing and began composing by voice while walking the city. The corpus that resulted is unlike anything he ever published. He died inside the practice. He is the absent center; the story does not flash back to him alive.
- **The narrator (unnamed for now).** His literary agent of thirty-one years. Mid-fifties, possibly older. Female. Practical, exhausted, fluent in the workplace texture of publishing. She knew him better than any spouse did. The story is her arrival at a body of work that was made in her absence by a writer she did not entirely recognize.
- **The phone.** A specific model — to be committed in Act II. The voice-transcription app, its quirks, its mishearings, the particular cadence of how it interpolates punctuation. The phone has a battery state, a screen lock, a recording timestamp. It is to be characterized as a lover is characterized — through what it does to the person who loves it. Not anthropomorphized. The intimacy is the intimacy of a tool that has shaped a hand.
- **The corpus.** The body of transcribed text. Should be characterized as having its own attentional dialect — speech-paced, with the rhythms of a man walking, with the algorithm's small betrayals retained. The corpus is what survived the man.
- **The super.** Established in scene 1. A finisher-of-sentences for tenants who never listen.
- **Other figures.** To be introduced only as the structure demands: a publisher's young assistant who has been waiting for the book; possibly an ex-spouse or grown child; possibly the retired waiter Marek had taken personally. Avoid populating the story with literary-world types; one or two will do.

## Commitments

- **Chandler grammar.** Short declarative sentences detonated by a long one. The simile that startles by being wrong-and-then-right. Attentional precision aimed at a single ordinary object until it becomes ominous. The wisecrack that is, on second reading, a small wound.
- **The phone as love interest, not as gimmick.** The romance must be felt as a romance. The phone does what a beloved does: it changes who someone is, it isolates them from prior intimacies, it eventually destroys them. The frame is sincere. The trick is that the lover happens to be an object.
- **Voice transcription as texture, not just as plot.** When the corpus is read or quoted, its prose should have the cadence of a man speaking aloud while walking, with the algorithm's signature legible: punctuation in odd places, homophone errors retained, the occasional aside the speaker made to himself and the phone faithfully kept.
- **Contemporary fixtures named without irony.** Slack pings, e-reader screens, the air-quality index, the FedEx envelope, the dead author's still-loading author page, the phone's lock screen. Period markers are 2026 and stable.
- **Death as a weather system,** not a puzzle. The body is found early. The mystery is the room around the body and the corpus inside the phone.
- **One scene per draft pass.** Do not advance the plot in synopsis.

## Refusals

- **No fedora, no rain-slicked alley, no femme fatale lighting a cigarette.** Pastiche kitsch is the failure mode.
- **No Marlowe-voice tics in proper-noun form** — no Bay City, no Velma. Borrow the grammar, not the props.
- **No detective procedural beats.** No interviewing witnesses one by one. No clues laid in numbered sequence. No reveal in the courtroom sense. The mechanism of death is revealed by reading the transcript, not by deduction.
- **No omniscient pivot.** The narrator only knows what she can see, smell, hear, and remember.
- **No sentiment about books, writing, or the writer's life.** Especially this. The literary world is a workplace; it is described as one.
- **No technophobic moralism.** The phone is not the villain. The phone is the lover. The story is not a parable about screens.
- **No flashback to Marek alive.** He is the absent center. We may hear him via the transcript; we do not see him.
- **No LLM tells:** no "tapestry," no "delve," no tricolons of abstract nouns, no "in a world where," no thesis-statement opening, no closing benediction.

## Attentional dialect

Look at things the way someone looks at things when they have stopped expecting the things to surprise them — and then let one thing surprise them anyway. The texture is observation that has gone past patience into something dryer. Imagery should be drawn from the material life of 2026: lithium batteries, courier apps, vacant retail, the smell of warmed laptop fans, dust on a router. When the phone or its transcript is on the page, language should briefly take on the cadence of dictation — half-thought, breath-broken, punctuated by an algorithm.

When in doubt: cut the second adjective. Then cut the first.

## Voice tests

- A sentence about a houseplant should be able to carry the weight of a death.
- A simile should make the reader hesitate for half a second.
- A piece of dialogue should leave more unsaid than said.
- A paragraph should end on the smaller of two available words.
- A transcribed passage should read as if it could have been spoken by a tired man at six in the morning, and the algorithm's small misreadings should feel inevitable, not cute.
